**1. Baseline exclusion versus (F1).**
(1) Claim attacked: Part II says two components are of one forward kind when “their signatures coincide on \(C\setminus\{(1,b_0)\}\). (2) Why it fails: Part V’s (F1) requires equality at *every* \((a,b)\in C\), and Part III says the contract “contains the baseline \((1,b_0)\).” So an anchored account cannot relate a component and its anchor that differ at \((1,b_0)\). The forward-kind exclusion is overridden by (F1); the stated rationale that components in different states can be one kind at baseline is not implementable in the anchored grade.  
(3) Seriousness: serious.  
(4) Fix: either exempt \((1,b_0)\) from (F1) for the anchored grade, or drop the “different states” rationale.

**2. Baseline exclusion is question-relative.**
(1) Claim attacked: “Only the question's baseline pair is excluded, on purpose.”  
(2) Why it fails: \(b_0\) is fixed by the question. Two components that differ only at \((1,b_0)\) are one forward kind for a question with baseline \(b_0\), but if a different question has baseline \(b_1\neq b_0\), then \((1,b_0)\) is included and the same components are different forward kinds. Kind-identity becomes dependent on which question is asked, not just on grain and contract.  
(3) Seriousness: serious.  
(4) Fix: define forward kind independently of the question’s baseline, e.g. exclude all identity-edit pairs \((1,b)\), or index kind to a fixed contract with a fixed baseline.

(2) Why it fails: if a boundary condition encodes position, state, or context, then a difference at \((1,b)\) for \(b\neq b_0\) is also a state difference under another boundary, not obviously a difference in kind. (3) Seriousness: serious.
