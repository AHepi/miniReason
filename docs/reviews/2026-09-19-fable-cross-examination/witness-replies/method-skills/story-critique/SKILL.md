# Story critique

This skill finds what is really wrong, and really right, with a story. It starts from single scenes, works outward to the whole, gives a reason for every claim, and offers a better version wherever a fault is serious.

It grew out of a long working session on one screenplay. The method is the writer's own way of looking at a scene, joined to John Truby's ideas about structure, and sharpened by David Deutsch's test that a good explanation is *hard to vary*: you cannot change its parts and still have it do its job. A story choice can be tested the same way, and so can each of your own notes. A worked example is in `references/worked-example.md`.

## The stance

**Never without reason.** Every point must run through four steps: what is on the page, what job it seems meant to do, why it does or does not do that job, and, for a serious fault, what would do the job better. If you cannot give the reason, drop the point. A critique without reasons is only taste, and the writer cannot act on taste.

**A note must be held in place by this page.** Test each note before you give it. *Swap the story:* would this same note fit almost any script ("raise the stakes", "show, don't tell")? Then nothing on this page is holding it, and it is a stock note. *Flip the scene:* had the scene done the opposite, would you have found a fault in that too? Then the note is coming from you and not from the page. Keep a note only if you can say which line, scene or job holds it.

**Honest in both directions.** Say what works and why. It tells the writer what to protect. If the writer offers their own diagnosis, check it against the page and say plainly where you agree and where you do not. Agreeing to be pleasant wastes their time.

**Missing is not the same as left open.** Mystery can be the point. Before calling something a hole, ask whether leaving it open does a job: does the question it leaves feed the ending, or the dread, or the theme? There is a test. In a *working* open question, each possible reading is held in place by something on the page, and nothing on the page settles between them. In a *hole*, nothing on the page bears on any reading. If you cannot tell which you have, say so and ask. Do not "fix" it. When the writer tells you something was deliberate, take that as settled and re-check which of your other points still stand.

**Judge the story they are writing, not the one you would write.** Find out the intended type of story, the medium and the setting, and what each is for. A modern-day setting, for instance, may have been chosen so the audience needs no explaining and can give all its attention to one strange idea. Critique against that intent.

**Plain words.** Explain any craft term in one sentence the first time it appears, then keep using that same word. Show the concrete case before the general point.

## Before you start

1. **Read the whole work from the page.** Not from memory, and not from the writer's summary of it. Note scene numbers or chapter names so every claim can point somewhere.
2. **Check the writer's facts first.** If their question rests on a detail the page does not support, correct it gently at the very top. Often the correction makes their point stronger. A critique built on a wrong fact is worthless. Do the same with a reported symptom. "It feels flat": to whom, at which point, compared with what? Make sure there is a fault to explain before you explain it.
3. **Read any background files** (world rules, production notes). They tell you what is intended. Treat them as frozen background, not as things to critique, unless asked.
4. **Pin down three things:** the type of story and what that type promises its audience; the medium (film script, prose, stage, game); and anything the writer has said it is meant to be. See `references/genre-promises.md`.
5. **For a long work, agree the scope.** If none is given, do the opening sequence and the ending first, because those two must click together and most faults show there.

## The five passes

Work in this order. Each pass feeds the next, and the early passes stop you making grand claims about theme that no scene supports. The full question lists are in `references/lenses.md`; read it before your first critique in a conversation.

### Pass 1 - Scene by scene

For each scene ask:

- What is this scene's job? What is different at the end of it: a fact, a bond between two people, a question in the audience's mind?
- Why this place, this action, this picture? What is it meant to stand for, and does it?
- What does it mirror or contrast elsewhere in the story? Look at the physical side (up or down, slow or fast, effort or ease, control or none, open or shut) and the story side (many ways things could go, or one narrowed question). A mirror that only repeats is weaker than one that contrasts.
- What does it plant for later? What does it pay off from earlier?
- Does anything come back: an object, a sound, an action such as a knock at the door? List each return and what it brings with it.
- Is anything leaning on a stock idea (the foreign curse, the mad scientist) instead of doing work of its own?
- **Remove it.** If it were cut, which job would stop getting done? Try removing things in pairs as well: two details can each be removable alone and not together, because they are two routes to one job.
- **Swap it.** Replace the place, the action or the detail with a near neighbour (stairs for a shaft, forty rungs for fifty-one). Does every job still get done? If yes, that detail is free. If no, the job that fails is what holds it in place. Name it.
- Mark each choice that matters: **held** (say by which job), **free** (could be otherwise, and that is fine), **idle** (does no job), or **two routes to one job** (keep both only if each has a second job). A scene held by several jobs at once is what readers call inevitable. Tell the writer to protect it.
- Does any added detail undo the work of another? More is not always safe.
- Could a different version do more jobs at once? If so, sketch it and say exactly what extra work it does. A job only counts if it rules out other versions of the scene.

### Pass 2 - Sequences and the chain of questions

- Group scenes into sequences. How does each open (wide, many possibilities) and close (narrowed to a single question with a clear feeling attached, such as "what happened, and should I be afraid?")? The opening and closing of a sequence should click together like two halves. They need not be black and white opposites.
- Write down the question the audience is holding at the end of each sequence. Does it become the next sequence's opening? Mark every place where the question is swapped for an unrelated one. This often happens when the story changes type partway.
- List things planted and never collected, and things collected that were never planted.
- For each turn of the plot, name the shown cause, then remove that cause in your head. Would the turn have happened anyway? Then the cause is a label and the turn happens only because the story needs it. Flag that, and any lucky meeting that helps the hero.
- For explaining scenes: is each rule shown small before it matters big? How many rules arrive at once?

### Pass 3 - People and their choices

- A person in a story is what they do when things change, not the words used to describe them. For any trait the script claims ("brave", "controlling"), name a moment where that trait makes this person act differently from how someone else would. If there is none, the trait is a label.
- For the main character: what flaw is hurting them and the people near them? What do they want? Who stands in the way? What past event still drives them? Is the flaw shown in action early, before it matters?
- **The choice test.** For every major decision, list the real options the person had at that moment. One option is a forced move. Good against bad is a fake choice. Neither carries meaning. A true choice has two options that pull almost equally.
- **The inevitability test.** Look in the choice scene first: are two real options actually on the page, where the audience can see them? If not, the fault is there. If they are, ask whether this person, as shown, could have chosen otherwise, and point to the scenes that settle it. Those scenes are usually earlier. In some kinds of story, such as a mystery, they come afterwards and the choice gains its weight looking back. If you cannot find them anywhere, that is where the work is missing. This is why character work matters: it is what gives a choice its weight.
- **Flip the choice.** Had the person chosen the other option, what later in the story would be different? If nothing would, the choice is idle, however dramatic it looks.
- **The cost test.** Is the price of each big act felt (seen in a body, a meal, a face, a bond) or only stated?
- Every other character needs a job. Note anyone who is only cargo or furniture. **Swap two of them:** if two characters could trade places in every scene and nothing would change, then as far as this story goes they are one character. Merge them, or give one a job the other could not do.

### Pass 4 - Theme as a pattern

- Put the story's central problem as one plain question about how to act. For example: "What may you do to someone, to save them, without asking?"
- **The pattern count.** List every moment where any character meets that problem. Each main character should answer it in a different way.
- Which of those moments are made to face each other? Which are left out of the final reckoning?
- **The fair mirror test.** When the story treats two acts as equal, are they? Look for the true fault, which is often not the act itself but what came after it.
- Does the ending settle things, or leave them open, in a way that fits what this type of story promises?

### Pass 5 - How it all fits

- List the scenes where a plot event is also a move in the theme. If there are only one or two, the rest of the plot is carrying nothing.
- Do the rules of the world express the theme, or merely sit beside it?
- **The medium check.** Does each device work in this medium? A camera sees from outside; prose sees from inside a head. A mark on a body that a camera can read may be invisible to the person wearing it.
- Check the promises of the story's type against what the story delivers. Those promises are patterns drawn from past stories, so treat them as questions and never as verdicts. If a usual beat is absent, ask what job that beat normally does and whether this story gets the job done another way.

## The write-up

Use this shape unless the writer asks for another. Keep it in proportion to the work: a short film does not need ten pages of notes.

1. **Corrections first**, if any. One short paragraph.
2. **Scene-by-scene notes**, grouped by sequence. Give each note a short bold label. Each note runs: what is there, the job, why it works or fails, and a better version if the fault is serious. Include what works, and say what holds it in place, so the writer knows what not to touch.
3. **How it fits together.** One short paragraph: where plot, theme and people meet, and where they do not.
4. **The two or three faults that matter most**, ranked, so the writer knows where to start. If the work is strong, say so and do not invent faults to fill the list. One real fault is a complete list. For a strong work, also say what a reviser or adapter must protect.
5. **What I am unsure of.** What may be deliberate mystery and not a gap. What you judged from the page alone, such as pacing in an unfilmed script.
6. **One next step.** One, not a menu.

If the writer wants it as a review, as if the finished work existed, tell the story first, then what works, then what worries you, then a verdict. Say plainly that you read a script and did not watch a film.

## After the critique

When the writer answers back, record what they say was deliberate, in their own words, and do not argue about intent. Then re-check your points against it. If the intent has changed, that is a new brief: say so, and judge against the new one from here on. Hold your own standard still in the same way. Do not judge one scene by one yardstick and the next by another.

If they ask you to rewrite: carry every finding forward; give each big choice two real options and let the person's shown nature decide it; keep a count of any scarce things in the story (tools, chances, ways out) so the ending still costs something; and finish with a short list of traps, meaning the places where a small later change would quietly break something else.

## Traps

These are the ways this skill goes wrong in use.

- **Critiquing from memory.** Misremembered details produce confident, wrong notes. Re-read the scene before you comment on it.
- **Fixing deliberate mystery.** If the writer meant it to stay open, a "fix" destroys it. Ask first.
- **Swapping in your own taste.** "I would have done it differently" is not a fault. "It does not do the job it set itself" is.
- **Piling on.** Twenty equal notes bury the two that matter. Always rank.
- **Praising to soften.** Only praise what you can give a reason for, the same as blame.
- **Letting an unequal mirror pass.** Stories love to say "she did what he did". Check that she really did.
- **Judging prose by film rules, or the reverse.** Run the medium check on every device you praise or fault.
- **Looking in only one place for a weightless choice.** Check the choice scene for two real options first. Then look earlier, and in some kinds of story later, for where the person's nature should have been shown.
- **Stock notes.** A note that would fit any script is not held by this one. Run the swap-the-story and flip-the-scene tests on your own notes.
- **Calling a free detail a fault.** Some details could be otherwise and do no harm. Only flag a free detail when the script presents it as if it mattered.
- **Cutting both of two routes.** Each looked removable alone. Together they were doing a job.
- **Treating the usual beats of a story type as rules.** They are patterns from past stories. Ask what job the beat does.
- **Copying the craft books.** Use their ideas in your own words. Do not paste passages from them into notes or files.
