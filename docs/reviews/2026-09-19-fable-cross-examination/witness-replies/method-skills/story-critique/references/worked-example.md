# Worked example

This file shows the note format and each test in use, on one short film script. It is cut down from a real session. Read it for the shape of the notes, not for the story.

**The script in four lines.** Iona breaks her brother Eli out of a medical factory. Their freight cage falls; Eli secretly uses a small engine that flips the cage so it comes back rising, and they survive, but as mirror images of themselves who no longer fit the world. A creature from a neighbouring world collects everything that was flipped, including Eli. At the end Iona saves the creature the same way, flipping it without asking.

---

## 1. Correction first

The writer asked: "Why does it start with someone crawling through vents?"

> One correction first, because it changes your example. The script doesn't open with crawling. Iona hangs in a vertical shaft and drops three metres onto a trolley, so it already opens with a fall. I think that makes your point stronger: the opening is a small copy of the later fall, which repeats the idea where it should contrast with it.

Why this matters: the writer's instinct was right and their memory was wrong. Checking the page turned a shaky note into a firm one.

## 2. A scene note in four steps

> **The unquestioned rescue.** Five rescues in this film are done without asking. *(what is on the page)* The first, Iona taking Eli, is the only one nobody questions; Eli simply wants out. *(the job: it should plant the film's question)* So the theme does not start until a third of the way in. *(why it fails)* If he hesitated, because he had his own plan with the flask, the question would be planted in minute two. *(a better version)*

## 3. The writer's own note, showing the mirror-and-contrast lens

> A vertical shaft where the character climbs upwards would contrast and mirror the most important scene: climbing up with great effort as opposed to falling with minimal effort. Moving up slowly with intention, purpose and control, against moving down quickly without any. An opening rife with possibility against a closing that narrows to a single question with a clear value signal: what happened, and should I be afraid.

In the rewrite this one change did four jobs: the contrast; an earned catch (she rests on the very sill she later grabs); a flaw shown early (she sees the missing brake and tells nobody); and a closing question for the sequence.

## 3b. The remove and swap tests on that opening

| Try this | What happens | Mark |
|---|---|---|
| Remove the climb; start in the corridor | The fall has nothing to contrast with, and the catch is luck | The climb is doing work |
| Swap the shaft for a staircase | Slow, effortful, upward: the contrast job is still done. But she never touches the sill, so the catch is no longer earned | *This shaft* is **held**, by the job "earn the catch" |
| Swap "fifty-one rungs" for forty | Nothing changes | The number is **free**. What is held is that she had *counted* them, which shows she rehearses the body's part of a rescue |
| Remove the chipped tooth | She still hides the warning tag, so "she conceals harm" is still shown | **Two routes to one job.** Keep the tooth only if it does a second job. It does: it gives her one dry joke with the friend before the night turns |
| Swap her trade (lift mechanic) for firefighter | She can still climb. She can no longer be the only one who knows how bad the tag is | Her trade is **held**, by the job "she knew the risk and told nobody" |

Four jobs hold the climb in place at once. That is why it feels like the only possible opening, and why the writer should protect it.

## 4. The choice test

> Neither flip is a real choice. Eli had no other move, and Iona has no other move. A dilemma needs a path the character could have taken and didn't.

The fix came from options the story world already allowed:

- **Eli:** the engine was built to carry a flask. It could have saved one person for certain, or all three on a knife-edge sum.
- **Iona:** let the creature go back to its own falling ship unflipped, or keep it and flip it. She also has ten seconds in which she could show it a picture and ask.

## 5. The inevitability test

> He bet on all three because he knew she would have climbed back down for the others. It was the one sum about her he had never got wrong.

> She had never in her life put down a thing she was carrying.

First the choice scenes were checked: after the fix, two real options are on the page in each. Then the proof of who each person is was looked for, and here it is planted early. Each choice is now settled by who the person is: she carries her brother's weight in scene two, she picks up a dropped pistol "the way you pick up anything a child might find".

## 6. The cost test

> This is the biggest problem in the script. Iona asks "Are we dying?" and never gets an answer, and neither do we. Nobody goes hungry, sickens or weakens. The cost of the flip is stated but never felt.

Fix: food that does not feed her; mint that tastes of rye bread; and at the end, her brother's face looking slightly wrong to her for good.

## 7. The fair mirror test

> Eli's fault was never the flip. It was "afterwards": staying silent until a recording exposed him. Iona has no afterwards, because she cannot speak to the creature at all. So when the film treats the two acts as equal, it lets Eli off cheaply.

Fix: give Iona her own afterwards. She hands Eli her helmet recording unasked and says the part nobody made her say: the flip also put her right.

## 8. The pattern count

| Who | Meets the question how | Faces whom |
|---|---|---|
| Iona | Carries people out; hides the risk from them | Eli, at the end |
| Eli | Decides by arithmetic; tells late | Iona, in the recording scene |
| The doctor | Contains; gives true answers smaller than the question | Nobody, in the script. A gap. |
| The creature | Takes without asking, then tends what it took | Cannot be asked anything |
| The friend | Wants to be asked | Nobody, in the script. Cargo. |

Two gaps found, both fixed with a few lines.

## 9. Missing, or left open?

The critic had listed as a fault that a key irony (the flask may itself have been flipped) was carried only by a glance. The writer replied: "it's meant to be ambiguous."

Right response: record it as a decision in the writer's words, leave the glance alone, and add a trap: "Nobody in the story says out loud whether the flask was flipped. Do not add a line that settles it."

## 10. The medium check, learned the hard way

The critic suggested a chipped tooth as a left-and-right marker. It would work for a camera. In prose, from inside Iona's head, it cannot: a flipped person's own mouth still feels right to her. In prose the marker became the backwards street signs instead.

## 11. Testing one of the critic's own notes

An earlier version of this skill said: "When a choice feels weightless, the missing work is almost always earlier." Put through its own tests:

- *Flip:* had the fault been in the choice scene itself (options never stated), the note would still have pointed at the earlier scenes. A note that gives the same advice whatever it finds is not being held by what it finds.
- *Where it came from:* fitted, on this one script, plus one author's say-so.
- *A swap that works:* in a mystery the person's nature is often shown afterwards.

So the note was reworded: check the choice scene for two real options first, then look earlier, and in some kinds of story later. The lesson is general. A critic's notes need the same testing as the story.

## 12. The close of the write-up

> I judged this from the page only. Some of what I call missing, especially the creature's reasons, may be deliberate mystery. The missing cost and the unexplained ship failure are not.
>
> The next step I'd suggest is to rewrite scenes 1 to 3 with your climb and with Eli hesitating, then check whether the ending lands harder.

Note the shape: what is uncertain, what is not, and one next step.

---

## Traps

- This example comes from one script in one type of story. Copy its shape, not its findings.
- The fixes shown here were made with the writer's agreement over several turns. Do not rewrite a writer's story unasked.
- Section 9 shows a note being withdrawn when the writer said the effect was deliberate. That is the right outcome, not a defeat.
