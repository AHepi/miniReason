# Lenses: the full question lists

This file says what the full questions for each pass are, and why each one is asked. Read it before a first critique. You do not need to answer every question in the write-up. Ask them all, then report only what matters.

Contents: 1 Scenes. 2 Sequences and questions. 3 People and choices. 4 Theme as a pattern. 5 How it fits. 6 Testing your own notes. 7 Borrowed ideas, in brief. 8 Traps.

A thread runs through all of it. David Deutsch's test of a good explanation is that it is *hard to vary*: change a part and it stops doing its job. A story choice can be tested the same way. Remove it, swap it for a near neighbour, flip it, and see which jobs stop getting done. What stops you swapping a thing is what holds it in place.

---

## 1. Scenes

**What is the scene's job?**
Why ask: a scene that changes nothing is decoration. Name what is different at its end. If two scenes do the same job, one of them is spare.

**Why this place, this action, this picture?**
Why ask: the writer chose a shaft, a kitchen, a bridge. A strong choice stands for something in the story and also does practical work. Ask what it is meant to stand for (being alone, struggle, a trap) and whether an audience would feel that without being told.

**What does it mirror or contrast?**
Look for pairs across the story. For each pair compare:

- direction: up or down, in or out, towards or away
- speed: slow or fast
- effort: hard-won or handed over
- control: chosen or done to them
- space: open or shut, wide or narrow
- story state: many ways it could go, or one narrowed question

Why ask: when an early scene and a late scene are built as a pair, the second one lands harder, because the audience's body already knows the first. A pair that contrasts (a slow climb by hand, then a fall in a second and a half) does more than a pair that repeats (a small fall, then a big fall). Contrast need not be total. The two halves only need to click together and push the plot on.

**What does it plant? What does it pay off?**
Why ask: a payoff with no plant feels like cheating. A plant with no payoff feels like a broken promise. Keep two running lists as you read.

**Does anything come back?**
An object, a sound, a repeated action. List every return and what arrives with it each time. Why ask: a run of returns that grows worse each time (a bang at the gate brings a guest, a knock brings a bill, a caller brings a death, a knock brings the thing itself) is structure doing the work of dread, and a reviser needs to know not to break the run.

**Is anything leaning on a stock idea?**
The curse from a far-off land, the scientist who went too far, the wise old helper. Why ask: a stock idea saves the writer effort but does no work of its own, and some of them have aged badly. Ask what job the device is doing and whether something particular to this story could do it better.

**Remove it. What stops getting done?**
Why ask: this is the quickest test of whether a scene or detail earns its place. "Texture" is an allowed answer, but only once or twice in a story. Remove things in pairs as well as singly. Two details can each be removable alone and not together, because they are two routes to one job (she hides a chipped tooth; she hides a warning tag; both show she conceals harm). Two routes is not a fault. Keep both only if each also does a second job.

**Swap it for a near neighbour. Does every job still get done?**
Swap the place (stairs for a lift shaft), the action (walking in for climbing in), the number (forty rungs for fifty-one), the object, the order. Use neighbours a fair writer might really have chosen, not silly ones.
- If every job still gets done, the detail is **free**. Free is fine. Most numbers, names and colours are free. Only flag a free detail when the script treats it as if it mattered.
- If a job fails, that job is what **holds** the detail in place. Name it: "the climb must be up *this* shaft, because the job 'earn the later catch' fails on any other."
- Swapping often shows that what is held is more general than what was written. "Fifty-one rungs" is free; "she had counted them" is held.
Why ask: removing tells you whether a thing does any work. Swapping tells you whether *this particular* thing does it. A scene that survives every swap is decoration. A scene held by three or four jobs at once is what readers mean by inevitable, and the writer should be told to protect it.

**Does an added detail undo another's work?**
Why ask: more is not always safe. A comic beat can drain the dread the scene before it built. A line of explanation can close a question that a later scene needs open.

**Could another version do more jobs?**
When you see one, sketch it in a few lines and name the extra work. Example of naming the work: "A climb by hand would contrast with the fall; would let her rest on the very sill she later catches, so the catch is earned; and would show she plans the body's part of a rescue and never the talking part."

**Inside the scene**
- Who wants what from whom, right now?
- What is said, and what is held back? A person who answers a smaller question than the one asked is telling you who they are.
- Does the scene end on a change, or one line too late?

## 2. Sequences and questions

**Openings and closings**
A sequence is a run of scenes chasing one short-term aim. A good one opens wide and closes narrow. "Wide" means many things could still happen. "Narrow" means the audience is left holding one question with a clear feeling attached: hope, dread, shame.

Check that the opening and closing click together both physically and in story terms. Then check the whole work the same way: does the first sequence's closing question echo in the last image?

**The chain of questions**
Write one line per sequence: "The audience is now asking: ..." Then read the lines in order.

- Good: each closing question turns into the next opening.
- Warning sign: the question is replaced by an unrelated one. The audience has to drop what it was holding. This is common when a story changes type partway (an escape becomes a puzzle becomes a monster story).
- You do not have to ban a change of type. Ask only whether the new question grows out of the old one.

**Planted and never collected**
Common kinds: an enemy who simply stops appearing; an injury that is never looked at again; a secret date, letter or name that is hidden from a character and then never shown.

**Cause**
For each turn of the plot: what, that we saw, caused this? Then remove that cause in your head. Would the turn have happened anyway? If so the cause is a label, and the real cause is that the story needed it. Be hardest on the turn that forces the ending. If the ship fails, the bridge falls or the phone dies only because the ending needs it, say so.

**Lucky meetings**
Luck that hurts the hero is fine. Luck that helps the hero needs a reason. The back-street doctor who happens to run the secret programme needs a line that explains who sent the hero to her.

**Explaining scenes**
- Is each rule shown with a small, clear case (a toy, a coin, a game) before lives depend on it?
- Are the scarce things counted on screen before they are spent?
- How many rules arrive in a single scene? More than two or three and it turns into a lecture.
- Does the script tell us what a moment means when the scene should have shown it?

## 3. People and choices

**A person is what they do when things change**
Adjectives in a script ("brave", "controlling", "kind") are labels. For each trait that matters, find a moment where it makes this person do something another person would not. If you cannot, the trait has not reached the page. The same test finds spare characters: if two people could trade places in every scene and nothing would change, then as far as this story goes they are one person. Merge them, or give one a job the other could not do.

**The main character**
- Flaw: what habit of theirs is hurting their life and the people near them? The best flaws also hurt others, so the person is not a blameless victim.
- Want: the outward goal that drives the plot.
- In the way: who wants the same thing, or wants to decide the same thing, and so keeps colliding with them? The strongest person in the way is often someone they love.
- The old wound: what past event still steers them? Is it on the page, even in a line?
- Is the flaw shown in action in the first few scenes, in something small, before it matters in something large?

**The choice test**
At every major decision, write out the options the person truly had at that moment.

- One option: a forced move. It shows nothing about them.
- Good against bad: a fake choice. Anyone would pick the same.
- Two goods, or two harms, of nearly equal pull: a true choice.

If a decision fails the test, look for options the story world already allows. Often they exist and only need saying out loud. A tool built to carry one small thing could have carried one person safely instead of risking everybody.

**The inevitability test**
Start in the choice scene itself. Are two real options on the page, where the audience can see them? If not, the fault is right there. If they are, then with two real options on the table this person must still only ever pick one, because of who they are. Find the scenes that prove it. They are usually earlier. In some kinds of story, such as a mystery, they come afterwards and the choice gains its weight looking back. If the proof is nowhere, recommend work wherever it best belongs. The best result is when two people's natures together settle it: he bets on all three because he knows she would have climbed back down for the others.

**Flip the choice**
Suppose the person had chosen the other option. What later in the story would be different? If the answer is "nothing much", the choice is idle however dramatic it looks, and the story's ending is not resting on it.

**The cost test**
Look for the price in something you can see or feel: food that no longer feeds; a loved face that now looks slightly wrong; a wall between two people. If the price lives only in dialogue, anger about it will feel thin.

**Who benefits**
Check whether an act helps the person who did it. That makes a choice muddier, and usually better. Make sure the story knows it.

**Everyone else**
Give each other character one line: "Their job in the story is ..." Anyone without a line is cargo or furniture. They should be cut, merged, or given the job of saying the thing nobody else will.

## 4. Theme as a pattern

**Say it as a question about how to act.** Not "this is about trust", but "what may you do to someone, to save them, without asking?"

**The pattern count.** Go through every character and list each time they meet that question. Write how each one answers. You want different answers, not the same answer five times.

**Who faces whom.** Mark which answers are set against each other in a scene, and which are left outside the ending. An answer that never gets challenged is a loose thread in the theme.

**The unquestioned first case.** Look hard at the first time the pattern appears. It is often the only one nobody in the story questions, because it looks like plain heroism. Questioning it early plants the whole theme.

**The fair mirror test.** When the ending says "she has now done what he did", lay the two acts side by side:

- Did both have options?
- Did both know the cost?
- Did both gain from it?
- What did each do afterwards? Silence afterwards is frequently the true fault, and a mirror that leaves it out lets someone off cheaply.

**The ending.** Does it settle or stay open in the way this type of story promises? An open ending should still narrow to one clear question with a feeling attached.

**A working open question, or a hole?**
List the readings an open question allows. For each, find what on the page holds it in place. Then look for anything that settles between them.
- Each reading held by something, and nothing settling it: a **working** open question. Leave it alone, and tell the writer what is holding each side so they do not remove it by accident.
- Nothing on the page bearing on any reading: a **hole**. The audience is not torn between answers; it simply has not been given anything.
- One reading held and the others not: the question is already answered, and the writer may not know it.
If two readings cannot be told apart by anything in the story, do not pick one. Say that the page does not decide.

## 5. How it fits

- **Meeting points.** List scenes where a plot event is also a move in the theme. Few meeting points means the plot is a run of puzzles with the theme bolted on.
- **World and theme.** Do the rules of the world make the theme happen? A rule that says "you come back, but you no longer fit" is already a theme about the price of rescue.
- **The medium check.** For every device ask who can see it. A film camera stands outside people. Prose stands inside one head. A scar on the wrong side is a gift to a camera and useless from inside the person, to whom their own body still feels right. The reverse holds too: a whole city read backwards is cheap in prose and costly on screen.
- **Point of view.** Whose head or whose shoulder are we at? Does the story leave it only when it must?
- **The vanishing test.** Anything that matters in the first third and is absent from the last third needs a reason.

## 6. Testing your own notes

A critique is a set of explanations of why a story does or does not work. Those explanations should be hard to vary too.

- **Swap the story.** Would this note fit almost any script? "Raise the stakes." "Show, don't tell." "Make her more active." If so, nothing on this page is holding it. Either tie it to a line, a scene and a job, or drop it.
- **Flip the scene.** Had the scene done the opposite, would you have faulted that as well? (Too much explanation; not enough explanation.) Then the note comes from you, not from the page.
- **Where did the note come from?** *Built:* worked out from the jobs this story has set itself. *Fitted:* a pattern from many other stories, which is reliable where stories are typical and unheld where this one is unusual. *Borrowed:* it rests on a craft rule; name the rule so the writer can weigh it. *Asserted:* you just feel it. Feelings are worth reporting as feelings ("I lost interest around scene 9"), but do not dress them as findings.
- **Hold your yardstick still.** Judge every scene against the same stated intent. If the writer changes the intent partway through the conversation, say that the brief has changed and judge by the new one from there.

Why this matters: a writer cannot tell a held note from a stock one by looking. If you hand over both without marking them, they will spend a week fixing something that was never broken.

## 7. Borrowed ideas, in brief

**From David Deutsch** (*The Beginning of Infinity*): a good explanation is hard to vary. Change a part and it no longer does its job. Here it is applied to story choices and to critical notes. The finer tests (remove in groups, two routes to one job, free against held, a working open question against a hole, fitted against built) come from the user's own theory, "Claude Fable Semantics". If the separate hard-to-vary skill is installed, use it when a single explanation needs testing in depth.

**From John Truby** (*The Anatomy of Story*, *The Anatomy of Genres*), restated in plain words. Use the ideas. Do not quote the books.

- **Seven steps of a story.** A person with a flaw; something they want; someone in the way; a plan; a final clash; a moment where they see themselves truly (or fail to); a new settled state. Use it as a checklist for what is missing, not as a mould.
- **The old wound.** A past event that still drives the person. In horror it matters most of all.
- **The person in the way wants the same thing.** Conflict is strongest when two people compete over one goal, not when one merely blocks the other.
- **Everyone is a version of the theme.** Each main character should handle the central problem differently.
- **The final choice narrows the theme to two.** The two options in the hero's last big choice are the two values the whole story has been weighing.
- **Two people can learn from each other.** A story may give the person in the way their own moment of seeing clearly, so the audience gets two insights.

## 8. Traps

- Offering only silly swaps. A swap must be a near neighbour a fair writer might have chosen.
- Calling a detail idle after removing it alone. Remove in pairs before you advise a cut.
- Flagging every free detail. Most details are free, and that is normal.
- Picking a winner between two readings the page does not decide between.
- Treating Truby's steps, or any craft rule, as a mould. They are borrowed patterns. Ask what job each step does in this story.
- Skipping section 6 because the notes "feel right". That feeling is exactly what the two tests are for.
