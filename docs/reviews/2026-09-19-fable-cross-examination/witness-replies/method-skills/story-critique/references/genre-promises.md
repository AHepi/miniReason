# What a type of story promises

This file says what horror and science fiction promise their audience, and how to work out the promises of any other type. Each type of story makes promises. A critique should check them, because a story can be well built and still let its audience down by breaking the promise they came for.

**How to hold these lists.** They are patterns drawn from many past stories. A pattern of that kind is reliable where a story is typical and tells you nothing where it is unusual. So use each item as a question, never as a verdict. If a usual beat is missing, ask what job that beat normally does for the audience, and whether this story gets that job done some other way. A story that breaks a pattern on purpose, and still delivers the feeling its audience came for, has not failed.

Only two types are written up here, because those are the two that have been studied closely for this skill. They draw on John Truby's *The Anatomy of Genres*, restated in plain words. For any other type, use the last section.

---

## Horror

**The promise:** fear, and under it a look at what is human and what is not. The best horror makes us afraid of the not-human in ourselves.

Things to check:

- **An old wrong that was never put right.** Something in the past keeps pressing on the present. Is there one? Is it tied, through the hero's flaw, to the horror that comes?
- **A closed place.** A house, a ship, a ward. It should feel like the hero's own fear made into walls. Does the story keep returning people to the same rooms?
- **The monster as the hero's double.** The thing we fear should share the hero's flaw in a purer form. If the hero carries people off to save them, so does the monster.
- **A calm, clever helper who explains.** A doctor or scientist who makes the impossible believable by doubting it first. Check they are not only a mouth for rules. A helper who turns out to work against the hero adds force.
- **A line that should not be crossed, and the price of crossing.** Someone cheats death or nature, and comes back with something missing. The price must be real and must grow. If nobody pays, it is not horror.
- **Pressure that keeps rising.** Tighter spaces, no help, injury, and attacks that come faster towards the end. Count the quiet stretches. One long stretch of talk in the middle lets the pressure out.
- **The final fight in a place that seemed safe.**
- **Little or no learning.** Horror heroes often fail to see themselves clearly. If yours does learn something, keep it small and costly.
- **A second ending.** The threat seems over, and then a last image says it is not. It works best as a question, not a jump.

**Existential horror** is the quiet branch. The fear comes from the size and indifference of things, from ordinary life going slightly wrong, from not being able to ask or be told. Check that the dread is carried by plain things (a street sign, a meal, a face) and that nobody is ever told "you are safe now".

**The strongest move when horror is joined to science fiction:** partway through, the thing we feared turns out to be the most humane one in the story, and the humans turn out to be the danger. Check whether the story makes this turn, and whether the humans' behaviour is ever called to account.

## Science fiction

**The promise:** one changed rule about the world, followed honestly to see what it does to people and to how they live together.

Things to check:

- **The one strange idea, paid for once.** State the rule plainly, early, and never add to it in secret. Everything after should be forced by that rule. Look for late, convenient additions.
- **How much world has to be explained.** Setting the story in the present day means the audience needs no teaching about the world and can give all its attention to the one strange idea. Flag any invented detail that pulls attention away from that idea.
- **A hero with a real flaw.** Science fiction heroes are often blank because the world is so large. The flaw should touch on what it means to be human.
- **Those in power as the ones in the way.** A company, a programme, an official. Check they do not simply drop out of the story once the adventure starts.
- **Several people in the way, tied to each other.** This lets the pressure keep coming back from different sides.
- **Everything funnelling to one narrow place and moment** for the final clash.
- **The biggest reveal arriving as a choice.** The hero is made to choose between two futures. Run the choice test on it.
- **Minor figures and other kinds of being.** Do they show a different way of living, or are they only odd to look at?

## Any other type

If the story is a love story, a crime story, a comedy or anything else:

1. Ask the writer what kind of story they mean it to be, if they have not said.
2. Name the feeling the audience comes for (fear, laughter, longing, the pleasure of working it out).
3. Name the question that type always asks. Love: can these two become better by being together? Crime: will the wrong be answered for? Detective: what is true?
4. List four or five things the audience will be waiting for, from your own knowledge of well-known works of that type.
5. Check the story against that list, and say in the write-up that the list is your own reading and not a fixed rule.

When a story mixes types, one of them should hold the structure and the others should serve it. Name which one is doing the holding. If the answer changes from sequence to sequence, that is probably where the chain of questions breaks.

---

## Traps

- Marking a story down for lacking a usual beat without asking what job the beat does.
- Applying the horror list to a story that only borrows a horror mood, or the science fiction list to one that only has a gadget in it. Ask the writer which type is holding the structure.
- Treating these two lists as complete. They cover what was studied closely for this skill, and no more.
