# The idea in depth

This file says what "hard to vary" means in full, and gives the reason behind each step of the procedure. Each section starts with a concrete case and then gives the general point.

Contents: 1 What "explains" claims. 2 The five checks inside "still explains". 3 Which parts do the work. 4 Jobs and reach. 5 Labels, and "the same at this level". 6 Direction, and what evidence can hold in place. 7 Blocks and absences. 8 Where a part came from. 9 Holding the question still. 10 When a test fails. 11 Near enough. 12 What this is not. 13 Traps.

---

## 1. What "explains" claims

**Case.** A pole stands in the sun. Its shadow is as long as it is because of the pole's height and the sun's angle. Saw the pole shorter and the shadow shortens. Wait for the sun to climb and the shadow shortens. Paint the ground where the shadow falls and nothing happens to the pole or the sun.

**Point.** To explain something is to lay out **parts** that depend on each other, and to claim a **match-up** between those parts and the parts of the real thing. The match-up is checked in only one way: by **what changes when things are changed**. Not by whether the parts have the right names. Not by whether the final number comes out right once.

That is why an explanation always comes with a **change list**, said or unsaid: the changes to the world it claims to keep matching through. No change list, no test.

---

## 2. The five checks inside "still explains"

"Hard to vary" asks whether a changed version of the explanation *still explains*. That phrase hides five separate checks. A version survives only if it passes all five.

**Check 1 - Each part moves as its counterpart moves.**
*Case.* A table that lists "on Monday the shadow was 2 metres, on Tuesday 1.9" gets every number right. Saw the pole and nothing in the table responds. It has no part that stands for the pole.
*Point.* For every working part, and every change on the list, the part must respond the way the matching piece of the real thing responds. A list of results has no such parts. (A table that truly recorded how the thing responds to every change on the list would be a different matter. It is the missing response, not the table shape, that fails.)

**Check 2 - The whole moves as the whole moves.**
*Case.* Every gear in a drawing of a clock is correct, but the drawing leaves out the shaft that joins two of them. Each piece checks out. The assembled clock in the drawing keeps different time from the real one.
*Point.* Pieces that are right one by one can still lose something they share. The reverse also happens: the whole comes out right while the pieces are carved wrongly, and then Check 1 fails. You need both.

**Check 3 - It answers the question that was asked.**
*Case.* Asked "what caused the bridge to fall?", someone shows beautifully that, under the building code, the bridge counted as unsafe. True, and an answer to a different question.
*Point.* Hold the question still. An answer to a neighbouring question is not an answer to this one, however good.

**Check 4 - The answer is not hidden among the starting points.**
*Case.* "Opium makes you sleepy because it has a sleep-making power." The thing to be explained has been put in at the start under a new name. Moving it into a box marked "law" or "nature" does not help.
*Point.* There must be some change on the list that removes or replaces a working part, leaves the rest alone, and changes the answer. If nothing you could change would change the answer, the parts are not producing it.

**Check 5 - The change list is not empty, and says what it leaves out.**
*Case.* "Our method always works - on the cases where it applies." Which cases those are is decided after each failure.
*Point.* The change list must contain changes that could have mattered. Anything possible that it leaves out must be left out openly, as a stated limit. A narrow claim, honestly marked, is fine. A claim narrowed in silence is not.

## 3. Which parts do the work

**Case.** A cake recipe: flour, eggs, sugar, butter, "stir clockwise", baking powder, self-raising flour.

- Remove "stir clockwise": same cake. That part is **idle**.
- Remove baking powder: the cake still rises. Remove self-raising flour instead (use plain): it still rises. Remove both: flat. Each is removable alone, and together they are not. They are **two routes to one job**.
- Add a cup of vinegar "for extra lift": ruined. An added part **broke** something that worked.

**Point.** To find which parts do the work, take them away and see if every job still gets done.

- Removing parts one at a time is not enough. Try pairs and groups. A group can be needed when no single member is.
- Two routes to one job is not a fault. Just report it as it is: each helps, neither is needed alone.
- More detail is not automatically safe. A version with an extra part can fail where the plain one worked.
- Sometimes no small set of parts is "the" support: many small limits together squeeze the answer to one value, and no single one does it. Report the group.

A part that can be removed with no loss is the first sign of an explanation that is easy to vary: if it can go, it can also be swapped for anything.

## 4. Jobs and reach

**Case.** The goddess story does one job: winter comes each year. Give it a second job, opposite seasons in the south, and no version of it survives. The tilt does both jobs unchanged, and also a third: long summer days near the poles.

**Point.** Every job you add can only shrink, or leave the same, the set of versions that survive. It can never grow it. That is why an explanation that reaches further is harder to vary.

Two cautions.

- **Counting is not a warrant.** Ten jobs that rule out no rival version hold nothing in place. One job that rules out most of them holds a great deal. Always ask of a new job: which versions did this kill?
- **Repeats add nothing.** Weighing the same thing on the same scale a hundred times tells you nothing the first weighing did not. A different, independent check can. "We've seen it work fifty times" is fifty repeats unless the fifty differ in a way that could have caught it out.

## 5. Labels, and "the same at this level"

**Case.** "Sales fell because of *market forces*." Swap in "*customer sentiment*". Nothing anywhere would look different. The label was doing no work.

**Point.** A part is what it *does when things are changed*, and nothing over that. Calling something "the cause", "a law", "an instinct", "the algorithm" adds no holding power. The test for any label: name a change to the world under which this label and a different one would come apart. If there is none, the label is decoration.

This cuts both ways. If two rival explanations respond identically to every change on the list, then **at this level they are one explanation**. That is not a failure to decide. It is the honest report that the question, as put, does not contain the difference. Anyone who says the two "really" differ owes you the change that separates them. Supplying it makes a finer question, which is a new question.

The same goes for telling a cause from something that merely goes along with it. The cause has a part that responds when you step in and change its input. The tag-along does not. No label settles that; only the poke does.

## 6. Direction, and what evidence can hold in place

**Direction. Case.** From the pole's height and the sun's angle you can work out the shadow. Run the same sums backwards and you can work out the pole's height from the shadow. Both get the right numbers. But saw the pole and the shadow changes; paint a longer shadow and the pole does not grow.
*Point.* Working backwards from a result is a fine answer to "what can we tell from what we see?" It is not an answer to "what produces it?" Direction is not read off the wording. It is found by poking each end.

**What evidence can hold. Case.** Two kitchen scales, each off by an unknown amount. Put the same bag on both. You now know exactly how far apart the two scales are. You still do not know what the bag weighs, and weighing it again, a thousand times, will not tell you. Deciding "scale B is dead accurate" because that gives the weight you hoped for is not something the readings told you.
*Point.* Some details cannot be settled by the evidence in hand, however much of it there is. For each detail ask: *could* what we have seen have told us this, even in principle? If not, the detail is loose until something else holds it, such as an independent check. Choosing it because it gives the answer you favour is going in a circle, even if the choice happens to be right.

## 7. Blocks and absences

**Blocks. Case.** Twenty-three coins cannot be shared equally among three people if coins cannot be cut. Whatever order you hand them out in, it never comes out even.
*Point.* "Why can't it happen?" is answered by finding something that no allowed step changes. Such answers are very hard to vary: the only way round is to change what steps are allowed (cut a coin), and that is a different question. It does not knock down the first answer. One caution: two things matching on that unchanging quantity does *not* show you can get from one to the other.

**Absences. Case.** "Why does this medicine have no effect on the heart?" A good answer shows that the route by which it could act on the heart is not there, and that if you added the route, there would be an effect.
*Point.* An explanation of why something is absent is tested by putting the missing piece in. If adding it changes the answer in the explanation as it would in the world, and leaving it out changes nothing in either, the explanation holds. This is the least settled part of the source theory, so treat such cases with extra care.

## 8. Where a part came from

**Case.** A baby watches a ball roll behind a screen.

- A guesser tuned only on what it has *seen* expects nothing behind the screen, because nothing is visible there. When the ball rolls out the other side, it is caught out. Tune it on more rolling balls and it is still caught out, because no amount of tuning on what is visible gives it a part that stands for a ball that is out of sight.
- A different move is to *build* a new part: "a thing keeps existing and keeps moving while hidden." Now the ball's return is expected. Nobody had to declare that part to be "an object". It behaves as objects do under every change on the list, and that is all being an object comes to here.

**Point.** Every part, and every match-up, got there in one of three ways.

- **Fitted.** Tuned by trial and survival on past cases. It is held in place exactly where it was tested. Everywhere else, other versions that would have survived just as well say something different. "It has always worked" tells you about the cases it met and nothing about the ones it did not.
- **Built.** Worked out with the thing itself in view, put up as a guess, and criticised. It commits to what happens under changes nobody has tried yet, so it can be caught out there. That exposure is what makes it hard to vary.
- **Asserted.** Someone wrote it down. It holds nothing until it is tested.

The three are told apart by their history, not by their results. Two explanations can give the same outputs today and differ completely in what holds them.

Being caught out by a change you were never tuned on is *surprise*. There are two replies to it. Re-tune (learn). Or build a new part (create). If every re-tuning still fails, the fault is in the parts you have, not in their settings.

## 9. Holding the question still

**Case.** "The new layout made sales fall." Sales then also fall in the region that never got the new layout. "Well, I meant it made them fall *more*."

**Point.** While a test is running, three things stay fixed: what is being explained, the change list, and what is asked. After the test they may change. People do find better questions, and finding the right question is as creative as answering one. But the changed version is a **new claim**, made at a new moment, and must be recorded as one. It does not reach back and make the old claim right.

Moving the goalposts is not the act of changing the question. It is changing it *without saying so*.

---

## 10. When a test fails

**Case.** The forecast said rain; it stayed dry. What failed: the theory of weather, the readings fed in, or the assumption that the instrument was working?

**Point.** A failed test knocks down the whole bundle (the explanation, the background taken for granted, and how the test was set up) and nothing narrower. Which piece to change is a fresh choice, and the hard-to-vary test helps make it: changing a part that has other jobs will break those jobs, so the cost shows at once. Changing an idle part costs nothing and teaches nothing.

Three more rules of fair play:

- A sign that something is wrong is not yet a criticism. It becomes one when someone lays out *how* it bears on the explanation.
- Taking away a supporting reason removes the support. It does not make the conclusion false.
- Missing evidence stays missing. Not having found a counter-case is not the same as having evidence for.

---

## 11. Near enough

**Case.** A map that is off by one step in every hundred is fine for crossing a field and hopeless for crossing a desert.

**Point.** If the match is only approximate, say so, and find out how the error grows as steps pile up. If you cannot say how it grows, you cannot say anything about the long run. Never let an exact question be quietly replaced by a rough one.

---

## 12. What this is not

- **Not truth.** A false explanation can be hard to vary. Its virtue is that it sticks its neck out and can be caught. A true one can be guessed for bad reasons and still pass every check; the reasons are judged separately.
- **Not a probability.** No number comes out of this.
- **Not elegance.** A long, ugly explanation that passes the five checks explains as fully as a short, pretty one. A shorter proof is nicer. That is not a sixth check.
- **Not depth.** How deep an explanation goes depends on the question. A proof that answers "why does this follow from these rules?" is complete for that question and silent on "what caused this?"
- **Not for taste.** Whether a thing is beautiful or good rests on reasons of another kind, which this test does not supply. What it *can* test is a claim that a creative choice does certain jobs.

---

## 13. Traps

- Treating "I can't think of a swap" as "no swap exists". Ask someone else to try, or try the opposite.
- Forgetting the change list and testing only whether the story fits what already happened.
- Treating two routes to one job as idle parts and cutting both.
- Accepting a finer question as if it rescued the old claim.
- Declaring a winner between two explanations that nothing on the list can tell apart.
- Letting the word "because" stand in for a working part.
