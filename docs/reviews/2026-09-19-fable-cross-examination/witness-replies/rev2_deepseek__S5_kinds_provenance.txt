## A. The exclusion of only \((1,b_0)\)

**Finding 1 — \(L_j(1,b_0)\) is a relation, not a “state.”**
(1) Claim attacked: “Only the question's baseline pair is excluded, on purpose: what a component imposes at the baseline is, as a gloss, its *state*…”
(2) Why it fails: \(L_j(a,b)\) is a relation on the ports of component \(j\), not a valuation or state. A baseline relation can be the only thing distinguishing two components, and it can impose a constraint that does explanatory work at the baseline. Calling it “state” does not make it non-structural.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: either do not exclude \((1,b_0)\), or define state formally as a valuation and prove that baseline relations never carry kind-relevant structure. As written, the exclusion is a stipulation that hides a structural difference.

**Finding 2 — The baseline exclusion drives a wedge between fidelity and kind.**
(1) Claim attacked: “(F1) … for every \((a,b)\in C\)” versus “their signatures coincide on \(C\setminus\{(1,b_0)\}\).”
(2) Why it fails: Fidelity checks the baseline pair; forward-kind identity ignores it. So the same pair is simultaneously required for account-hood and declared irrelevant to kind. A component can be “one forward kind” with its anchor while the relation at the baseline is exactly what makes the account right or wrong.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: either include \((1,b_0)\) in the signature relation, or state and defend a separate notion of “state difference” and show it cannot be explanatory.

**Finding 3 — The baseline exclusion makes the analysis-of-kinds conjecture vulnerable.**
(1) Claim attacked: Part XV: “A case where a kind-label distinguishes two accounts that no change in \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) distinguishes and no history distinguishes…”
(2) Why it fails: If two components differ only at \((1,b_0)\), they are not of different forward kinds because the baseline is excluded. If their histories are the same, they are not of different historical kinds either. A label “has baseline relation \(R_1\)” versus “has baseline relation \(R_2\)” discriminates them, and if the question is about the baseline answer, that distinction does explanatory work. That refutes the conjecture unless “state labels” are excluded by fiat—and no such exclusion is stated.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: either include the baseline, or explicitly restrict the conjecture to non-state labels and define state labels.

## B. Provenance of a content when several transports witness (R)

**Finding 4 — “The provenance of a content's occurrence” is a type error.**
(1) Claim attacked: “The provenance of a content's occurrence is the provenance of the transport that witnesses (R) for it; where several do, the earliest.”
(2) Why it fails: A content is abstract; an occurrence is a carrier. (R) relates an occurrence \(o\) to a content \(c\) via some \(D\) and some \(t\). “Several” could mean several \(D\), several \(t\), several occurrences, or several bindings. The phrase does not specify what is being counted or whose provenance is being assigned.
(3) Seriousness: fatal for the definition as stated.
(4) Fix: define provenance for a representation token \((o,D,t)\) or for a binding occurrence, not for “a content's occurrence.”

**Finding 5 — The earliest-witness rule lets an earlier selected transport mask a later construction.**
(1) Claim attacked: “where several do, the earliest.”
(2) Why it fails: Suppose content \(c\) already has a selected transport \(t_1\). Later the system constructs a new transport \(t_2\) to the same \(c\). Both witness (R). The rule assigns \(c\)'s occurrence the provenance of \(t_1\), i.e. selected. Then a component realized by \(t_2\)'s binding is classified as a selected historical kind, not a constructed one, even though it was constructed. This breaks historical kinds, Build, Origin, and the claimed asymmetry of Grievance 12.
(3) Seriousness: fatal for the construction/selection distinction.
(4) Fix: assign provenance per transport or binding occurrence, or if per content, choose the witness actually deployed/operative, and allow multiple provenances with conflict rules.

**Finding 6 — “Several transports” can be faithful on different contracts.**
(1) Claim attacked: (R): “Faithful_{C_c}(t:c→D)” plus “where several do, the earliest.”
(2) Why it fails: \(t_1\) may be faithful on \(C_c\), while \(t_2\) is faithful on a larger contract. Both witness (R). The earliest may be the less reliable or less comprehensive transport. The rule does not respect the contract in force or the deployment context.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: define representation relative to a contract and choose the witness faithful on that contract, or state that provenance is independent of current deployment.

**Finding 7 — Declared transports do not witness (R), so the rule is silent about them.**
(1) Claim attacked: (R) requires \(\operatorname{Sel}(t)\lor\operatorname{Con}(t)\).
(2) Why it fails: If an occurrence has a selected, a constructed, and a declared transport, the declared one is ignored entirely. If the declared transport is the one actually used in modelling or deployment, the provenance assignment can misrepresent the system’s actual representational relation.
(3) Seriousness: minor to serious depending on use.
(4) Fix: distinguish provenance of physical representation from provenance of the modeller’s assertion, and state which is being assigned.

## C. “Earliest” under a partial order

**Finding 8 — “Earliest” is not well-defined in a partial order.**
(1) Claim attacked: “the provenance of the **earliest** witness in \(\prec_h\) whose events produced the occurrence that carries \(t\).”
(2) Why it fails: \(\prec_h\) is explicitly “acyclic precedence,” i.e. a partial order. A partial order can have multiple minimal elements. If two incomparable witnesses—one selection, one construction—both produced the occurrence, there is no unique earliest witness. The same problem infects “where several do, the earliest” for content provenance.
(3) Seriousness: fatal for the provenance rule.
(4) Fix: require a unique minimal witness, impose a total order, or define provenance as a set of minimal witnesses with a stated conflict rule.

**Finding 9 — A witness is not an event, so its position in \(\prec_h\) is undefined.**
(1) Claim attacked: same “earliest witness” passage.
(2) Why it fails: A selection witness is a tuple \((\mathcal T,\mu,H,\Sigma_h)\); a construction witness is \((h,e)\). A selection witness contains a history \(\Sigma_h\) with many events. Which event gives the witness its position? The earliest event? The latest? The event that produced the occurrence? None is specified.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: identify a single producing event inside each witness and order witnesses by that event, or define a witness-level order explicitly.

**Finding 10 — “Whose events produced the occurrence” is causal but the occurrence carrying \(t\) is undefined.**
(1) Claim attacked: “whose events produced the occurrence that carries \(t\).”
(2) Why it fails: A transport is defined as a tuple of maps \(t=(\pi,\tau,\sigma,\lambda)\). A tuple of maps is not an occurrence and has no location or causal history. The text later says “A transport is an occurrence in a physical history,” but that contradicts the earlier definition. “The occurrence that carries \(t\)” is therefore undefined.
(3) Seriousness: fatal for the provenance of transports.
(4) Fix: distinguish transport type from transport token; define a token as an occurrence realizing the mapping, and assign provenance to that token.

## D. History, fidelity, and the operative condition

**Finding 11 — The operative condition is both inside and outside the anchored grade.**
(1) Claim attacked: Part V: “it is stated outside the anchored grade so that the asymmetry of Grievance 12 holds exactly: forward kinds and anchoring are history-free, and the operative condition is not.” Versus Part VI: “the anchored grade's active-route condition uses it.”
(2) Why it fails: If Part VI is correct, active-route history is part of the anchored grade. If Part V is correct, Part VI is wrong. Either way, the document contradicts itself about whether anchoring is history-free.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: state explicitly whether the operative condition is a conjunct of anchored account-hood or a separate predicate, and correct every passage that disagrees.

**Finding 12 — Part I's “only the operative condition … looks at history” is false.**
(1) Claim attacked: “only the operative condition of Part V looks at history, and it says so.”
(2) Why it fails: (R) requires selected or constructed provenance. Historical kinds are predicates on history. Deploy, Build, Origin, Newness, EK, active routes, and question provenance all use history. Part V itself says “Provenance enters at (R), at Deploy, and at (EK).”
(3) Seriousness: serious overclaim.
(4) Fix: replace “only” with a complete list of history-dependent predicates.

**Finding 13 — The baseline pair can carry history into fidelity.**
(1) Claim attacked: (F1) requires equality at every \((a,b)\in C\), and \(C\) contains \((1,b_0)\).
(2) Why it fails: If \(L_k(1,b_0)\) encodes a state produced by the carrier’s history—memory, learned weights, damage, calibration—then fidelity at \(C\) depends on that history. The document calls this “state” and excludes it from forward kind, but F1 still checks it. So fidelity is not history-free if state is history-encoded.
(3) Seriousness: serious for the history-free claim.
(4) Fix: either prove that baseline relations cannot encode history, or acknowledge that fidelity is state-dependent and may therefore be history-dependent.

**Finding 14 — \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) is occurrence-specific and may be history-dependent.**
(1) Claim attacked: Part XII: “for each occurrence, the **admitted edit set** \(A_\Theta(o)\).”
(2) Why it fails: Anchoring condition 3 quantifies over \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}=A_\Theta(o)\times B\). If \(A_\Theta(o)\) depends on the occurrence’s state or history, then world-kind and anchoring are indexed to that history. The document never states that \(A_\Theta(o)\) is history-independent.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: require \(A_\Theta(o)\) to be determined by module type, grain, and current substrate state—not by provenance history—or explicitly index anchoring to the occurrence history.

**Finding 15 — The anchored grade does not require F2 or (A) on \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\).**
(1) Claim attacked: “Anchoring is therefore component fidelity on the full admitted contract, with a proper decomposition at the question's resolution.”
(2) Why it fails: Condition 3 requires each active component to be of one forward kind on \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) with its anchor. It does not require the assembled organization to satisfy (F2) or question fidelity (A) on \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\setminus C\). So an anchored account can have a wrong assembly or wrong answer on an admitted change outside \(C\), while every active component matches its anchor.
(3) Seriousness: serious overclaim.
(4) Fix: add (F2) and (A) on \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) to the anchored grade, or restate the grade as component-level only.

## E. Is the analysis-of-kinds conjecture now refutable?

**Finding 16 — The conjecture is not well-posed because “historical kind” is underdefined.**
(1) Claim attacked: “A historical kind is a predicate on the history that produced an occurrence of a component — selected, constructed, descended from, copied from — with the witness that Part IV requires for the provenance of a content's occurrence, applied to the binding that realizes the component.”
(2) Why it fails: If “historical kind” means any predicate on history, then any two occurrences with different histories are of different historical kinds, and the conjecture is trivially true. If it means only the provenance categories selected/constructed/descended/copied, then many historical differences—e.g. two different construction methods—are not historical kinds, and the conjecture is false. The document does not specify which reading is intended.
(3) Seriousness: fatal for the conjecture’s status.
(4) Fix: define historical kinds as equivalence classes under a stated relation, not as arbitrary predicates, and specify whether only provenance types count.

**Finding 17 — The refutation condition “no history distinguishes” is not operational.**
(1) Claim attacked: Part XV: “A case where a kind-label distinguishes two accounts that no change in \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) distinguishes and no history distinguishes…”
(2) Why it fails: The document gives no criterion for when two histories are distinguished or not. Since histories are rich sets of occurrences with partial order, one can almost always invent a historical predicate that separates them. Without a canonical equivalence relation on histories, the refutation condition cannot be checked.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: state exactly which historical predicates count, and give a decision procedure or at least a clear criterion for “no history distinguishes.”

**Finding 18 — Baseline-only differences make the conjecture refutable if state labels count as kind-labels.**
(1) Claim attacked: same Part XV refutation condition.
(2) Why it fails: As in Finding 3, two components differing only at \((1,b_0)\) are neither different forward kinds (baseline excluded) nor different historical kinds (if same history). A label that distinguishes them can do explanatory work at the baseline. If such a label counts as a kind-label, the conjecture is refuted. The document does not exclude this case.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: either include the baseline, or explicitly classify baseline labels as state labels and exclude them from the conjecture by definition.

## F. Additional serious defects in the surrounding machinery

**Finding 19 — The non-fallible-selection refutation in Part XV is impossible under Derivation 3’s hypothesis.**
(1) Claim attacked: “A selection history \(H\subsetneq C\), in a population closed under pointwise alteration of component relations off \(H\), whose survivor is determined on \(C\setminus H\). This refutes Derivation 3 under its hypothesis.”
(2) Why it fails: Derivation 3 says that if some admissible \(L'\) changes the solution set at \((a,b)\in C\setminus H\), then there is a surviving rival that differs there. If the survivor is determined on \(C\setminus H\), then no admissible \(L'\) changes the solution set—or the population is not actually closed. So the alleged refutation is not a refutation; it is the case where the antecedent of Derivation 3’s conditional fails.
(3) Seriousness: serious logical error.
(4) Fix: remove this as a refutation; state it as a boundary condition under which selection may determine off-\(H\) behavior.

**Finding 20 — The protective-contract refutation in Part XV contradicts the definition of objectivity.**
(1) Claim attacked: “A stated scope, passing Part V's objectivity condition, that nonetheless excludes an admitted change on which the candidate is evaluable and wrong.”
(2) Why it fails: Part V defines \(C\) as protective exactly when there is an admitted pair \((a,b)\in C_{\mathrm{adm}}\setminus C\) on which the candidate is evaluable and wrong. Objectivity requires non-protection. So no scope can both pass objectivity and exclude such a change. The alleged counterexample is impossible by definition.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: rephrase the attack. For example, show that the condition is too weak because it only checks evaluable changes and thus silence can hide wrongness, or show a non-protective scope that omits a change on which the candidate is evaluable and wrong—impossible as stated.

**Finding 21 — The selection witness’s non-triviality clause is incoherent.**
(1) Claim attacked: “Non-triviality: no event of \(\Sigma_h\) instantiates an organization held to \(t\), to \(H\), or to the survival condition by a faithful transport.”
(2) Why it fails: \(t\) is a transport, \(H\) is a set of edit–boundary pairs, and a survival condition is a predicate. None is an organization. “Held to” is defined as a relation between organizations. The clause is therefore undefined.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: rewrite non-triviality in terms of organizations representing the transport’s mappings, or drop the clause.

**Finding 22 — (R) and the surprise/violation section are inconsistent.**
(1) Claim attacked: (R) requires “Faithful_{C_c}(t:c→D)”; later: “Surprise at \((a,b)\in C\) is a failure of fidelity of *that* transport on \(C\), so that transport no longer witnesses (R) for the content on \(C\).”
(2) Why it fails: If \(t\) was faithful on \(C_c=C\), then fidelity cannot fail at any \((a,b)\in C\). If it was not faithful on \(C\), it never witnessed (R) on \(C\) in the first place. The text tries to rescue this by saying (R) uses the largest contract the transport is in fact faithful on, but (R) as written uses the content’s contract.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: define representation relative to an actual faithful contract and allow contract shrinkage, or separate the content’s declared contract from the transport’s faithful contract.

**Finding 23 — Build requires a prior failure, excluding construction without prior error.**
(1) Claim attacked: “\(\operatorname{Build}\) … contains an **itemised defect representation** — an occurrence instantiating a record pairing a state at which some prior transport of \(s\) failed fidelity with what would have held — on an active route to the binding.”
(2) Why it fails: This makes construction necessarily error-driven. A system can construct a new organization or transport in response to a question, a conjecture, or a creative act without any prior transport having failed fidelity. Such cases are excluded by definition, yet the document claims construction is where creativity lives.
(3) Seriousness: serious for the account of creativity.
(4) Fix: allow construction prompted by a question or conjecture without prior transport failure, or argue explicitly why failure is necessary.

**Finding 24 — Non-circular dependence may require an edit that \(\tau\) does not supply.**
(1) Claim attacked: “there exists an edit \(\tau(a)\) on \(E\), translated from some \((a,b)\in C\), that removes or replaces a nonempty **proper** sub-block \(B\subsetneq\Gamma\)…”
(2) Why it fails: The witness must be an edit in the image of \(\tau\). If the only way to remove \(B\) is an edit on \(E\) not translated from \(C\), a genuinely non-circular account fails the condition. The condition thus tests availability of translations, not non-circularity.
(3) Seriousness: serious.
(4) Fix: allow edits on \(E\) not in \(\tau[C]\) for the non-circular witness, provided they preserve the relevant boundary conditions.

**Finding 25 — The category error about transports infects several definitions.**
(1) Claim attacked: “A transport is an occurrence in a physical history: it came to hold by selection, construction, or declaration…” versus the formal definition “\(t=(\pi,\tau,\sigma,\lambda):D\to E\).”
(2) Why it fails: A tuple of maps is not an occurrence. The document repeatedly treats \(t\) as both a mathematical relation and a physical process: “the occurrence that carries \(t\),” “held to \(t\),” “the organization \(t\) runs from.” This is not a local slip; it undermines transport provenance, content provenance, historical kinds, and the selection/construction distinction.
(3) Seriousness: fatal for the provenance machinery.
(4) Fix: define transport types and transport tokens separately. Assign provenance to tokens, not to tuples. Rewrite every passage that conflates them.

**Findings.**

**1. Baseline exclusion is underinclusive and ad hoc.**
- Claim attacked: “Only the question’s baseline pair is excluded, on purpose: what a component imposes at the baseline is, as a gloss, its *state*… the identity edit at the baseline separates nothing, while the identity edit at a different boundary is a response to a change of boundary and does separate.” (Part II)
- Why it fails: The exclusion removes only \((1,b_0)\). But \((1,b)\) for \(b\neq b_0\) is also an identity edit, merely at a different boundary. If \(B\) encodes environmental or system state, then \((1,b)\) can carry exactly the kind of state information the exclusion of \((1,b_0)\) is meant to suppress. The gloss “what a component imposes at the baseline is its state” is also false as stated: \(L_j(1,b_0)\) is a component *relation*, not a valuation. Two different constraints can have the same solution state but different \(L_j(1,b_0)\). The definition excludes a relation, not a state.
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: define state coordinates explicitly and exclude those coordinates from kind signatures; or include \((1,b_0)\) and separate state from kind by a projection onto non-state variables. Do not rely on “baseline” as an informal state marker.

**2. Excluding \((1,b_0)\) loses law-like differences, not only state.**
- Claim attacked: “Two subnetworks \(U,U'\) … are of one forward kind … under which their signatures coincide on \(C\setminus\{(1,b_0)\}\).” (Part II)
- Why it fails: Take \(L_1(1,b_0): x=0\), \(L_2(1,b_0): x=1\), with \(L_1,L_2\) identical on all other pairs. They impose different constraints at the baseline. The definition makes them one forward kind. If the baseline pair is part of the question, this erases a real difference in what the components impose. Calling that difference “state” is not justified by the formalism.
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: either include the baseline pair in kind signatures, or give a formal state/kind distinction that shows why the baseline relation is merely state. A worked example would be required.

**3. The boundary/edit distinction is conflated.**
- Claim attacked: “the identity edit at a different boundary is a response to a change of boundary and does separate.” (Part II)
- Why it fails: \(C\subseteq A\times B\). A boundary \(b\in B\) is not an edit; \((1,b)\) records the component relation at boundary \(b\), not a response to a change from \(b_0\) to \(b\). The signature stores \(L_j(1,b)\), not a difference \(L_j(1,b)-L_j(1,b_0)\). If boundary conditions can encode state, then \((1,b)\) can separate components by state under a different boundary label. The exclusion of only \(b_0\) is therefore arbitrary.
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: distinguish edit coordinates from boundary coordinates formally; if boundary changes are to count as admitted changes, model them as edits in \(A\) and say so. Otherwise, exclude all identity-edit boundary pairs or none.

**4. Historical kinds are ill-defined.**
- Claim attacked: “A historical kind is a predicate on the history that produced an occurrence of a component — selected, constructed, descended from, copied from — with the witness that Part IV requires for the provenance of a content’s occurrence, applied to the binding that realizes the component.” (Part II)
- Why it fails: Part IV’s provenance witness is a witness for a *transport* that witnesses representation of a *content* by an *occurrence*. A binding \(\lambda(k)\) is part of a transport; it is not a content occurrence and has no (R)-witness. “Descended from” and “copied from” are not defined provenance categories in Part IV, which defines only selected, constructed, and declared. “Predicate on the history” is so broad that any historical difference can be made a historical kind, which trivializes the analysis-of-kinds conjecture.
- Seriousness: fatal for the conjecture’s precision.
- Fix: define historical kind as an equivalence class under a specified provenance relation on binding tokens, using only defined provenance categories with explicit witnesses. Remove undefined categories or define them.

**5. Transports are category-mistaken for occurrences.**
- Claim attacked: “A transport is an occurrence in a physical history: it came to hold by selection, construction, or declaration, and its provenance is a fact about events.” (Grievance 12) and “the provenance of the earliest witness … whose events produced the occurrence that carries \(t\).” (Part IV)
- Why it fails: A transport is defined as a tuple \((\pi,\tau,\sigma,\lambda):D\to E\). It is not an occurrence; occurrences instantiate organizations. Relations do not come to hold in the way physical occurrences do, and they are not “carried” by an occurrence. Part IV then predicates provenance of \(t\), but provenance is defined via “the occurrence that carries \(t\),” which is undefined. The same ambiguity recurs in \(\operatorname{Sel}(t)\), \(\operatorname{Con}(t)\), and (R).
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: distinguish transport type from transport token. Define provenance for the token/occurrence that implements the transport. If \(t\) is a type, define \(\operatorname{Sel}/\operatorname{Con}\) for tokens, or define a set of provenances for the type.

**6. Provenance of a content is not well-defined when several transports witness (R).**
- Claim attacked: “The provenance of a content’s occurrence is the provenance of the transport that witnesses (R) for it; where several do, the earliest.” (Part IV)
- Why it fails: Several transports can witness (R) for the same occurrence–content pair. They may have different provenances: one selected, one constructed. The earliest-witness rule arbitrarily picks one. If an earlier selected transport exists, a later constructed transport that actually introduces or deploys the content is ignored; if the two are simultaneous or incomparable, no earliest exists. Also “content’s occurrence” is unclear: content is an organization plus commitments, not an occurrence.
- Seriousness: fatal for provenance attribution and for the claim that creativity lives in construction.
- Fix: define provenance per transport token and per introduction event. For a content occurrence, define provenance as the provenance of the event that introduced that content into the repertoire, not of any transport that happens to witness representation.

**7. (R) contradicts the “largest contract it is in fact faithful on” remark.**
- Claim attacked: (R) requires \(\operatorname{Faithful}_{C_c}(t:c\to D)\), but Part IV also says “what it represents by (R) is the content on the largest contract it is in fact faithful on, which may be smaller than the contract declared for the content.” (Part IV)
- Why it fails: If \(t\) is not faithful on \(C_c\), it does not satisfy (R) for content \(c\) with contract \(C_c\). If it is faithful only on a smaller \(C'\), then by the semantics’ own contract-indexing it represents a different content \(c'\) with contract \(C'\), not \(c\). The sentence either contradicts (R) or changes content identity without saying so.
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: either require (R) only on the largest contract actually satisfied and define content as contract-indexed, or delete the sentence. Do not say both.

**8. “What (R) takes from outside is \(\operatorname{Inst}_\ell\)” is false.**
- Claim attacked: “What (R) takes from outside is \(\operatorname{Inst}_\ell\).” (Part IV)
- Why it fails: (R) also uses \(\operatorname{Faithful}\), which depends on the organization data and the contract, and \(\operatorname{Sel}\lor\operatorname{Con}\), which depend on physical histories, populations, variation operators, survival conditions, episodes, and construction witnesses. These are not supplied by \(\operatorname{Inst}_\ell\) alone.
- Seriousness: minor to serious, because it hides the semantics’ external commitments.
- Fix: list all external inputs of (R): \(\operatorname{Inst}\), the transport, the content’s contract, and the provenance witnesses for \(\operatorname{Sel}\) and \(\operatorname{Con}\).

**9. “Earliest” is not well-defined under a partial order.**
- Claim attacked: “the provenance of the earliest witness in \(\prec_h\) whose events produced the occurrence that carries \(t\).” (Part IV)
- Why it fails: \(\prec_h\) is an acyclic precedence order, not necessarily total. Witnesses are sets or episodes, not points. There may be multiple minimal witnesses, incomparable witnesses, or witnesses whose first events and last events are ordered oppositely. “Earliest” is not defined for such objects. If two minimal witnesses have different provenances, the rule gives no provenance.
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: define a total order on witness occurrences, e.g., by completion time, or define provenance as the set of provenances of minimal witnesses. If uniqueness is required, state and justify the uniqueness condition.

**10. Later construction is wrongly classified as maintenance.**
- Claim attacked: “Later histories are maintenance and do not change provenance.” (Part IV)
- Why it fails: A later construction that builds a new binding or transport for the same content is not maintenance. Under the earliest-witness rule, an earlier selected transport can fix the content’s provenance as selected, erasing the later constructive act. This directly undermines the claim that creativity lives in construction.
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: make provenance token-specific and event-specific. A later construction witness should give the later token constructed provenance. Do not let an earlier representation of the same content overwrite it.

**11. The operative condition is ill-typed.**
- Claim attacked: “an anchored account is operative when each anchor is an active route (Part IX) — a subnetwork of actual occurrences.” (Part V)
- Why it fails: An anchor is a subnetwork of the target organization \(D\). An active route is a connected subnetwork of actual occurrences in a history. \(D\) is an organization, not a history. There is no defined map from \(D\)’s components to occurrences, so “each anchor is an active route” is a type mismatch.
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: define a realization map from the anchor subnetwork of \(D\) to a subnetwork of occurrences, and say the realization is an active route. Index the condition to an occurrence instantiating \(D\), not to \(D\) itself.

**12. The operative condition contradicts the anchored-grade claim about mechanism.**
- Claim attacked: Part 0 says the anchored grade is “what ‘the explanation matches the mechanism’ means at the module’s index.” Part V says “a decoy with the same response to *every* admitted edit … is, at this index, the mechanism.” But Part V also says an anchored account is operative only when each anchor is “not a backup that would have responded identically.”
- Why it fails: A backup that would have responded identically can satisfy the anchored grade but is explicitly not operative. If operative is required for being the mechanism, then the anchored grade alone does not match the mechanism. If operative is not required, then the operative condition is idle for explanation. The text cannot have both.
- Seriousness: serious.
- Fix: distinguish “signature-mechanism” from “operative mechanism,” and state exactly which one the anchored grade is supposed to capture. If the intended claim is operative mechanism, include the operative condition in the relevant grade and admit that it is historical.

**13. Operative is undefined when no history is supplied.**
- Claim attacked: “Where the physical module supplies a history for \(D\), an anchored account is operative when…” (Part V)
- Why it fails: For mathematical organizations, modelled organizations, and any \(D\) for which the module supplies no history, the predicate is not false; it has no truth value. If any later predicate uses “operative,” the semantics has a gap.
- Seriousness: minor to serious.
- Fix: make operative explicitly partial, or define it as false/not applicable when no history is supplied. State the consequences for assessments.

**14. The analysis-of-kinds conjecture is not rigorously refutable as stated.**
