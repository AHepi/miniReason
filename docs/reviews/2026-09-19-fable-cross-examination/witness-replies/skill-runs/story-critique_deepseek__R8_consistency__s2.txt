## 1. Corrections first

## 2. Notes

### Part IV — Occurrences, transports, provenance

**Note 1. The one orientation cannot mean both things it is made to mean at once.** (Your candidate 1; see also note 6.) The page first states the invariant:

> "**Orientation.** A transport from \(D\) to \(E\) is the relation by which \(E\) is *held to* \(D\): \(D\) is what \(E\) answers to. … This one orientation is used everywhere: an explanation \(E\) is held to its target \(D\) by \(t:D\to E\); a carrier's organization \(D\), with \(\operatorname{Inst}_\ell(o,D)\), is held to the content \(c\) it represents by \(t:c\to D\)."

In clause one the codomain is the more derivative thing (the explanation; the carrier's organization) and the domain is what it answers to. In clause two the codomain is the *content* and the domain is the carrier's organization — the reverse. Same notation, opposite status. The job of the passage is to fix this once; it fails at the second sentence.

**Note 2. The (R) direction conflicts with (F1)'s declared carrier side, not merely with a phrase.** (Your candidates 1 and 2.)

> "\(\operatorname{Rep}_\ell(o,c)\iff\exists D\,\exists t\,[\operatorname{Inst}_\ell(o,D)\land\operatorname{Faithful}_{C_c}(t:c\to D)\land(\operatorname{Sel}(t)\lor\operatorname{Con}(t))].\)"

(F1) is written over a transport \(t:D\to E\) as `proj_{V_k}[Sol_{λ(k)}(a,b)] = L_k(τ(a),σ(b))` — the codomain component relation is the *derived* side; the codomain components get anchors in the domain. Under `t:c→D` that says every port-domain and component of the carrier's organization is anchored in the *content*, and the carrier's component relations are the projections. If instead the author meant (R) to be `t:D→c` ("content is what the organization answers to" — the coherence reading), then λ is carried differently, `Sel`/`Con` still belong to the same relation, and nothing else in (R) changes. I cannot tell which is meant; the page does not decide. This is exactly the case where the semantics' own working-open-question test applies, and here the test returns **hole**, because nothing on the page bears on which of the two readings is intended *except* the sentence that asserts the opposite of what (R) writes. The fix costs one arrow and one sentence naming which object λ's components live in, on which side. Until that is settled, note 3 holds.

**Note 3. Derivation 8's λ-example only fits one of the two directions.** The proof excludes from the permitted φ the map that changes what a component is anchored to:

> "because \(\lambda\) is among the transported data, a recoding that would change what a component is anchored to — the pole-height port carried to the shadow-length port — is not among the permitted \(\phi\)"

In the explanation orientation, the pole's height is a port of the *target* \(D\), and the content to which the explanation is transported is *the explanation* — so the parenthetical is apt. Under (R)'s written orientation, \(c\) is the domain, \(D\) the codomain, and λ maps components of \(D\) into subnetworks of \(c\); "what a component is anchored to" is then a content subnetwork, not the pole. Derivation 8 supports the first reading only. This is the "flip the scene" test: had the document written the other arrow, (F1)'s side condition would not have been available as evidence either way, and the example would have read differently. **Built**, not fitted: it is held by the document's own two texts at once.

**Note 4. The precedence rule is not the same rule as ordinary priority.** (Your candidate 12, restated.) Provenance is "determined by its history … with the following precedence: it is *constructed* if a construction witness exists; otherwise *selected*…". A transport selected for years and then, in an episode satisfying \(Build\), given a construction witness, is *constructed*. Maintenance histories "after origination do not change provenance" — but construction *is* origination. This is a deliberate priority rule dressed as a factual finding about history. It is not a contradiction, but it means two clauses of Part IV — "its history is the fact" and "precedence says which" — are two different rules, and a reader cannot tell which is meant to govern a borderline case. Leave if deliberate; state as a rule if not. This is the same pattern as **Grievance 12**, which you should treat as settled.

**Note 5. The construction witness is made of material whose provenance the rule has just excluded.** (Your candidate 2 in its sharper form.) Constrained `Build` forbids compositions of content-preserving transfers; a transfer is content-preserving when its ends are \(\equiv_\ell\); the construction may operate on *incoming carriers*. Either the carriers are (a) bare occurrences, in which case "content-preserving" applied to its inputs is empty and the constraint is vacuous — this is the reading that makes the stratification in Part XIV work — or (b) already contents, in which case the first construction's inputs have contents that were never themselves constructed or selected, and the "no compositions" clause is quietly never satisfied for a genuine first construction. Only reading (a) is consistent with the stratification the document claims; the page never says which reading its authors intend, and Part XIV's guarantee that no predicate is undefined does not reach inside \(Build\)'s phrase "incoming carriers". Report and ask; do not fix.

### Part V — Account

**Note 6. The two-place Account is well motivated but the page never carries the witness into the places the document itself uses it.** (Your candidate 2; see also notes 16, 29.) The definition is clean and deliberately weak:

> "The predicate is two-place on the organization and the question, with the transport and commitments existentially quantified: \(\operatorname{Account}(E,p)\iff\exists t,\Gamma\ \operatorname{Account}(E,p,t,\Gamma)\)"

The job — "a modeller must be able to assess an account without settling its history" — is real and is protected. But three readers of the predicand cannot recover which \(t\) and which \(\Gamma\) were assessed, and the document itself needs them: (K1) assesses a *criticism's* organisation; (EK) assesses \(c\) "on the contract of \(p_c\) fixed at \(e_c\)" — a contract of a different question; and in \(Build\)'s clause "deleting the binding breaks (F1) or (A) for \(c\) on its contract", there is no \(E\), no \(t\), and no \(\Gamma\) in sight, yet (F1) is a relation between "every active component \(k\) of \(E\) with anchor subnetwork \(\lambda(k)\)" and its anchor. One content can pass via a fine \(E_1\) and fail via a coarse \(E_2\); until the content-level Account is given, the \(Build\)-clause is quantified over the wrong order. Fix: append to the two-place definition that every occurrence is short for a particular \(\mathcal E=(E,p,t,\Gamma)\), and give parts VI, IX, X and XI the witness they cite. Cost: one sentence. (This note survives the flip test: a document *without* the existential is the different document in which every occurrence already carries its witness; it fails the "assessed without settling history" job, so the note is held by that job, not by taste.)

**Note 7. The five conditions are six formulas, and the split matters downstream.** (Your candidate 3.) "holds exactly when the following **five** conditions hold" is followed by (F1), (F2), (A), NonCircular, NonVacuous, Adequate — six binary conjuncts under five headings, with (F1) and (F2) folded under "component fidelity", while the attack list in Part 0 does the opposite and names them separately:

> "Show that the properness and resolution conditions on anchors (Part V) … exclude a genuine mechanism or admit a decoy … each fail a named condition" / "(F1) or (F2)."

One page says two conditions, another says (F1) and (F2). The count is not cosmetic here because Part VI's restriction "deleting components … holds everything outside fixed" is evaluated through the (E) conjunct list, and the intended reading of (F2) as an independent conjunct controls how much a single component deletion is allowed to damage. Pick one presentation and correct the attack list to match. Cheap fix, but it should be done with the same care as a held detail, not at the end.

**Note 8. The qualification "fidelity … is over component structure under change, not over outputs" belongs to (F1) alone.** (Your candidate 3 in its stronger form; see note 12.) The Part 0 sentence

> "Fidelity in this semantics is over **component structure under change**, not over outputs. Two systems with identical outputs and different internal routes are different organizations here"

is true of (F1) — and false of (F2), which is precisely the assembly condition `π[Sol_D(a,b)] = Sol_E(τ(a),σ(b))`, an equality of output projections. Derivation 9 then proves that no function of the output projection can separate accounts that differ — which is the correct theorem and is *the reason (F1) exists*. So the page needs both: (F1) preserves structure, (F2) checks assembly at output level, and the disclaimer about outputs must be attached to (F1), not to "fidelity" as a whole. As written, the disclaimer is over-broad; it lets a reader think (F2) is about components. This is the cause-versus-label test: (F2) is not a redundant label on (F1) — it catches a lost shared constraint that (F1) cannot (the document says so), so it is not removable; but it is the output-level conjunct, and the disclaimer must not claim its work.

### Part II, Part V, Part XIV — Kinds, grains, baselines

**Note 9. The baseline exclusion is simultaneously too small and indexed by a set the contract need not contain.** (Your candidate 4; see also note 27.) On the page:

> "**of one forward kind on \((\ell,C)\)** when there is a bijection of their footprints under which their signatures coincide on \(C\setminus\{(1,b):b\in B\}\). The baseline pairs are excluded on purpose: the relation a component imposes at the baseline is its **state**"

Two faults, both "cause versus label". (i) The exclusion is \(C\setminus\{(1,b):b\in B\}\), but \(C=(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\cap S)\cup K\) contains the baseline pair \((1,b_0)\), not the family \((1,b)_{b\in B}\). So the excluded set is (a) over-determined — it removes pairs that are not in \(C\) — and (b) under-determined — for every \(b\in B\) the relation \(L_j(a,b)\) is still a state relation, and any pair \((a,b)\) with \(b\neq b_0\) still carries state into the kind comparison. The stated *purpose* — "two things at different positions are one kind in different states" — argues for excluding every pair whose response is the state, i.e. every pair at a non-baseline boundary as well; the *writer's stated exclusion* does not do it. (ii) Nothing in the page declares which boundary \(b_0\) is, so a change of declared baseline moves the partition of components into forward kinds, and unlike the grain the baseline carries no admissibility condition, no "new claim at a new index" rule, no provenance. (K) is indexed \((a,b,L_j)\), grain, and contract — but the baseline \(b_0\) is an index of the *question* \(p\) only through \(C\), and the kind definition reads \(B\) as a set of states from outside the question. This is the cleanest internal contradiction in the kind apparatus. It is not deliberate design in the sense Grievance 12 is; it is a missing index. Fix: either exclude all pairs with \(a=1\) in the stated form (making the exclusion uniform in \(B\)), or state that state-differences are to be represented at a finer contract and say which \(b_0\). I hold the *defect* to the page and offer both readings; which one the author wants is theirs.

**Note 10. Derivation 1's conclusion is stronger than needed and weaker than a kind-claim.** (Your candidate 4, the second half; see also note 28.) The proof's induction gives signature coincidence on \(\tau[C]\) — baseline included — and the result then says

> "a faithful component is of one forward kind, on \(C\), with its anchor subnetwork."

Forward-kind identity excludes baseline pairs, so the derivation secures *more* than kind-identity and is therefore weaker than the name "kind preservation" suggests for the label-elision conjecture; the document catches this itself ("What this does and does not show"). The good statement is present and should be protected: the result is about signatures, and the analysis-of-kinds conjecture is explicitly *not* claimed to be proven. This note is a *stronger* statement of your candidate: the theorem is fine; the title and the sentence "of one forward kind, on \(C\)" should be marked as the step that is *not* what the theorem proves to that audience, because the same sentence is what Derivation 10 later leans on for a kind-claim.

**Note 11. The grain-admissibility clause reaches non-physical organizations through a physical notion, and its "realizes" is undefined at the point of first use.** (Your candidate 5.)

> "A grain \(\ell\) is a declared index, but not a free one. A grain is **admissible** for an occurrence \(o\) when the physical module licenses an organization at that grain (Part IV, Inst) and every component of that organization has a signature that some physically admitted edit realizes"

The clause is for an *occurrence*, but the rescue sentence — "Claims can be rescued by moving to a grain only if the new grain is admissible" — is general, and for mathematical organizations (Part III's declared admission relation; Part VII's skew example) there may be no occurrence and no \(A_\Theta\) at all. Either admissibility is physical-only and the rescue sentence is scoped to physical organizations, or "admissible" has a declared counterpart for mathematical organizations, and the page does not name it. Also, "signature that some physically admitted edit realizes" uses \(L_j(a,b)\) on edits the contract \(C\) may not include, which is the correct reading (the signature is C-relative; the admissibility check quantifies over the module's edits) but is not said. Unlike your candidate list implies, this is not the strongest grain problem — the strongest is the missing baseline condition, note 9. One line fixes this one.

### Part IV, Part X — Surprise, Deploy, and the two routes

**Note 12. The entailment is right in substance and unindexed in statement.** (Your candidates 6 and 9.)

> "surprise at \((a,b)\in C\) is a failure of fidelity on \(C\), so on that contract the system has, by (R), stopped representing the content, and by Part X stopped deploying it"

Part X's Deploy is "\(\operatorname{Deploy}_{\beta,\ell}(s,c,\xi;U)\) holds when \(s\) at \(\xi\) holds a representation of \(c\)… integrated… as a retained capability." So the entailment must be "at the state after \((a,b)\)"; the sentence as written has no state index and no "for that transport". Read as "is not deployed at any state", it is false, because the same system can deploy the same content through a different organization or a retuned calibration (the "selection response" of Part IV, which re-tunes *within* the population a few lines later). Read as "this transport is un-faithful at that pair", it is true and important. The consequence is a real gain for the semantics, so the fix is to say which: either *this representation*, or with the state index. This is the "flip the scene" test for my note: had the document written the state-indexed version, I would have found nothing wrong, so the note is held by the missing index, not by taste. Severity: medium — it is a logical gap in a lemma-like sentence, not in a definition.

**Note 13. The two routes to the same job are named but the choice between them is never put to a test.** (Pass 2's "two routes to one job"; the choice test turned on the document's own decision point.) The passage

> "A **selection response** extends \(H\) and lets \(\mu\) act… A **construction response** introduces a new organization or a new transport with a construction witness. Only the second can be originative under Part X."

plants a real fork, and it is one of the best structural moves in the document. But the same paragraph asserts that the fork is settled by "only the second can be originative". If the selection response can never satisfy (G) — which is true by (G)'s definition, since (G) requires \(Build\) and \(Build\) requires a construction witness — then the sentence is a consequence, not a remark, and it is currently *stated* as if it were a separateness result. More importantly, the fork is never placed where the reader can see the two options pull against each other; Part IV's selection and construction responses are described, and Derivation 10 later runs the two options on one scenario, which is the place where the fork *does* get two real options. Fix: move the "only the second can be originative" claim out of the general paragraph and mark it as what it is (a consequence of (G)), and let Derivation 10 be the place the fork is shown. Held by the job "the two provenances are distinct", which the rest of the document protects.

### Part VIII, Part XVI — Frozen index and Derivation 7

**Note 14. The claim's identity is fixed by an object that the same claim is assessed against — the historical index needs a stateable object.** (Your candidate 7.)

> "A claim indexed to a contract keeps its identity when a later theory changes the current contract; its truth value at its own index does not change, and a claim at a new index is a new claim."

Part III says an assessment "is an event at a fixed \((C,\mathcal Q)\); any change to either ends that assessment", and Part V's account is two-place in \(p=(D,C,b_0,\mathcal Q,O_p,\rho_p)\). So the "claim" of the historical index is really a claim about a \(p\), and the page calls it a claim about a contract. That is a genuine ambiguity (not a contradiction): if the claim is "\(E\) is an account on \(C\)" — a phrase the document uses, e.g. "it is an account at that contract" — then it is not a full Account claim, since the account is two-place in the whole \(p\), and its identity depends on \(O_p\), \(\rho_p\), and \(b_0\), none of which is mentioned. Derivation 7 then proves consistency for the narrow reading only; it does not address the case where the later theory changes \(O_p\), which by Part III "ends that assessment" but which the historical index's sentence would wrongly say keeps identity. Fix: state "a claim indexed to a *question* (equivalently to its contract, \(\mathcal Q\), baseline and obligations) keeps its identity", and let Derivation 7 confirm it. This note is held by the document's own two-place definition and by the change-of-scope case at Part V's adequacy passage — which is the case that matters, because adequacy is exactly where a contract gets narrowed.

### Part XVI, Derivation 10, against Part XIV

**Note 15. The scenario does nothing the definitions forbid, and the membership classes are not what it claims to have joined.** (Your candidate 8.)

> "This is a scenario, not a consistency witness… no condition is checked on it."

Part XIV lists memberships by predicate — "the creative-episode class: base interpretations with an instance of (G) connected to a critical episode" — and Derivation 10 closes "It is not a claim that any actual infant, animal, or program has been shown to instantiate any of this." Those two are consistent. The tension is real but different: the scenario runs a *conditional* — "If (F1) and (F2) hold for \(t_1\) on the extended contract… then by Derivation 1 the persistence components are of one forward kind" — and it makes two *unconditional-looking* claims inside: "\(S_0\)… is faithful on \(H_0\)" ("assume… as a stipulation"), and "that is a violation off \(H_0\): surprise by definition". The scenario's own disclaimer covers the first; the second is a definitional instantiation and is fine. So there is no contradiction with Part XIV. But there is a live one with **Part XII**: the scenario stipulates a population of trackers, a variation operator, and a survival condition — a physical selection history — and says it is "a stipulation about that population", while Part XIV says membership requires "satisfying physical realization wherever a physical attribution is made" and Part XII says the selection witness "is a claim about a physical history under \(\Theta\)… fallible and checkable". An unchecked stipulation is not a witness, and the document states the general obligation itself. Add: the scenario lives in the possible-membership space, not in the class. This is exactly the "where a pass does not transfer" case in miniature — say it once and move on, which the scenario does. Severity: low, but it will be read as a claim of membership by every reader in a hurry, and it costs one clause to prevent.

**Note 16. (EK) names a contract from one question and checks an answer profile against another, and never says \(p_c=p_c\).** (Your candidate 2 as it lands in Part XI.)

> "\(\operatorname{Account}(c,p_c)\land c\in\operatorname{Result}(\Delta)\land\operatorname{Deploy}(s,c,\xi';U_c)\land\operatorname{ProducesVia}(\Delta,c,o;\xi,\xi')]\). Here \(c\) is a content whose organization is the \(E\) of \(\operatorname{Account}(E,p_c)\), and \(\operatorname{Account}\) is evaluated on the contract of \(p_c\) fixed at \(e_c\)"

Two things are said at once: (A) is against the query of \(p_c\), while F1/F2/NonVacuous/Adequate are against the contract of \(p_c\) (a different question). For these to be the same assessment, \(p_c=p_c\) — never stated. For a re-parameterised question this matters: the whole point of the merit condition is that a *found* question \(p'\) may have a new contract, and (EK) must be able to carry it; but then "the contract of \(p_c\) fixed at \(e_c\)" must be allowed to be the new question's contract, and "the new parameterisation counts as found" must not be assumed. Fix: state that \(c\) is assessed for \(p_c\), and only for \(p_c\); and where \(p_c\) is the found question, say the assessment is against the found contract. Cheap, and it protects (EK) from being gamed by exactly the move Part XV warns about.

**Note 17. The full (F1)/(F2)/(A) triple is invoked twice under a name that is not its own.** Derivation 2 and Derivation 8 each cite "satisfying (F1), (F2), (A)" (Derivation 2) and "(E), anchoring, (G), (P), (EK)" (Derivation 8). Derivation 2's claim uses (F1), (F2), (A) with "the anchored relations … coincide" — which is exactly the content of the first two conjuncts of (E) plus (A), but presented as if the whole of (E) were the hypothesis. This is not a contradiction; it is the same fold as note 7 making derivations rest on a weaker hypothesis than their labels suggest. Say "the first three conjuncts of (E)" and the notes stop mattering.

### Part IX–XVI — Provenance, construction, origin

**Note 18. The reference to \(N\) is careful, the \(Build\) clause is not.** Derivation 10's "then (G) holds" is claimed under "\(S_1\) is not in the prior repertoire", which is \(New\)'s condition; the derivation correctly cites all four conjuncts it uses. \(Build\)'s clause "deleting the binding breaks (F1) or (A) for \(c\) on its contract" is the only place in the document that does not name the witness \((E,p,t,\Gamma)\) it is judging, and it is the one place where a *binding* — a component of the built organization — is required to fail a conjunct, so it is holding note 6's whole weight. Either name the witness, or drop the "for \(c\) on its contract" and let it be "for the use" in the ordinary sense. One line.

**Note 19. The construction/selection boundary is stated in two ways and the pair should be one.** Part IV: "What keeps the two apart: a selection witness contains no itemised representation of a defect and no target instantiated before the variation; a construction witness contains both." Part X, ten pages later, says "Construction is not selection… a construction witness contains both." Part XV's attack (D) says the same thing again and asks for a *rewriting* between the two. The page does not say which of the two is meant by "the discrimination requirement of Part IV" in Part XV. If the discrimination is the *absence/presence of an itemised representation*, the two provenances are distinguished by a property of one witness, and there is no independent test; if it is a rewriting test, the definition in Part IV is the wrong kind of object (a *claim* about a witness, not a *property* of it). This is the load-bearing distinction of the whole document (Grievance 6, Part XV (D)); the two statements are near-identical enough that I cannot tell whether they differ. Ask the author, do not fix.

**Note 20. The free-and-held ledger.** For Pass 1's marks, the results of the remove and swap tests as they actually landed:

- **Held in place by several jobs at once, protect:** the *orientational slogan* "D is what E answers to" (Part IV) — held by (F1)'s carrier-side reading, by Derivation 8's λ-example, and by the explanation-case clauses of (F1)–(A); do not soften it, fix the (R) usage instead. The *NonCircular* clause (Part V) — held by the "\(p\) because \(p\)" exclusion, the one-component repackaging exclusion, and the Derivation-5 construction of \(D_p\), which is where the "new dimension" limit is paid for. The *declared admission relation* (Parts III, VII, XIV) — held by the skew example, the eliminative explanation's second case, and the whole "no physical module" caveat under attack (B).
- **Free, and should not be defended:** the *counts* "two primitive packages" (Part XIV), "three layers" (Part IV), "four cases" (Part XV (B)), "five conditions" (Part V). Three is a description of a modelling choice, not a theorem; the count is fine as prose but should never be cited as a result. The document does not do so — I checked — and this is right.
- **Idle, and should either be given a job or dropped:** the *approximation* (T2). "Without a modulus, no accumulated bound follows" is work, but (T2) is never used anywhere in the document, is listed in Part XV as a refutable result, and is not a semantics claim at all. Its job is unknown. Refit it to a question the semantics actually asks, or move it to a remark.
- **Two routes to one job, keep both:** *observation edits* (Part II) and *interventions on the measured port* (the measurement signature) both block the causal/measurement distinction, from opposite sides. Each also does a second job: observation edits give "report without altering" its content; the measured-port invariance gives identification its content (Part VII). Keep both, and Derivation 2 should say it uses only the ones it uses (note 17).

### Theme, medium, and the fair mirror

This is where the skill's Pass 4–5 transfers only in its weakest form, so I run it in the form it *does* take for a foundational document: the document has a **central promise** ("one changed view of explanation, followed honestly to what it does to kinds, causes and representation") and asks how each part answers it. I say this once and move on, as the skill's traps section instructs for an unusual type.

**Note 21. The pattern count has one answer, repeated three times, and one hard case asserted but never shown.** The "one-component repackaging of \(\operatorname{Sol}_D\)" is named three times — at attack (A)? no; at Part 0 "the one-component candidate whose single relation is the target's own solution set", at Part V "The one-component candidate that repackages \(\operatorname{Sol}_D\) satisfies (E). It is an account at \(C\) and not anchored", and at Part XV — and it is always the same case. The genuinely hard decoy — "(F) The anchored grade. Show that the properness and resolution conditions on anchors (Part V) either exclude a genuine mechanism or admit a decoy that shares a world-kind with the mechanism on every physically admitted change" — is listed as an *open attack*, not as an example. So the anchored grade's extra work is carried by one named candidate and one open challenge; the document has no worked case of a candidate that fails *only* condition 4. This is the "who faces whom" list of Pass 4: the two grades face each other once, on Sol_D. Add one decoy that passes (E) and fails 4 (the reversed calculation is the natural one — it is already stated for (F2); say that it fails 4 rather than only saying it is "not anchored"), and the pair's whole power becomes visible. Held by the document's own promise that it "separates a mechanism from a decoy that shares its signature on the contract someone happened to choose".

**Note 22. The unquestioned first case.** Pass 4's "the unquestioned first case" is, here, the primitive layer's transport, which is called "typically selected" and never itself run through the anchored grade. The three layers' transports are where creativity is supposed eventually to live; the document says "Selection produces the raw material construction works on" and never asks whether the *selection* itself is an account of anything. That is a stated limit ("None of the three is presupposed to exist"), so it is not a fault of the definitions — but it is the pattern the document leaves outside the reckoning, and the author should know it is the one place where the whole architecture is given rather than derived (Grievance 6). Say it, one line, in Part IV's third-layer paragraph.

**Note 23. The medium check.** A trivial one, but worth doing because the document is single-medium symbolic, and the skill demands the check be run rather than skipped. **Note 24. The fair mirror.** The one place the document says "these two acts are the same kind" is Derivation 10's swap:

**Note 27. ## 3. How it fits together

Where the parts meet: the through-line is *change-over-label* — (K), (F1), (R), and the two grades are four views of one relation, and Derivation 8 shows the whole apparatus is equivariant under exactly the recodings that preserve that relation. That is the strongest structural move in the document and should be protected. Where the parts fail to meet: (F1)'s carrier side and (R)'s written direction (note 2) meet only under a reading the document does not choose; the two-place `Account` is stated as the way "a modeller must be able to assess an account without settling its history" and is then used at three places that need the witness back (notes 6, 16, 18); and the kind-identity relation excludes baselines by a rule indexed differently from the contract it excludes them from (note 9). The theme ("what it is, is settled by what changes when things are changed") is carried by (E) and the anchored grade, and at exactly the two places the document calls open — symmetry and selection artefacts — the same theme is the part that does not reach. That is not a fault; it is where the author's stated reals are.

## 4. The three faults that matter most, ranked

1. **The one-orientation slogan and (R)'s arrow cannot both stand** (notes 1–3). It is the single sentence the whole representation apparatus rests on; every attribution of representation inherits it. Fix: one arrow and one sentence naming which object λ's components live in.
2. **The witness-free two-place Account** (notes 6, 16, 18, plus 27 and 30). It is not a definitional error; it is that four later conditions are written as though the witness were present, and the document itself needs it present. Fix: append "for a particular \((E,p,t,\Gamma)\)" wherever a conjunct-level claim is made, and give Part X's \(Build\) clause its witness.
3. **The baseline-pair exclusion** (notes 9, 10, 28). It is the deepest of the three, because it is not a slip: the same object (state) is excluded in one index and admitted in another, and no condition controls the choice of baseline. Fix by choosing between the two readings and saying which, in the same paragraph as the exclusion.

Honourable mentions, in this order: the \(O_p\)-less account (note 26), the \((G)\)–merit–\((P)\) cycle (note 29), and the unlisted \(A_\Theta\) index (note 25).

## 5. What I am unsure of

I am unsure whether the (R) orientation is a slip or an intended asymmetry with a different λ — the document uses "the same orientation everywhere", which argues slip, but a slip of the pen does not usually leave the surrounding proofs consistent with its consequences. I am unsure whether the baseline-pair exclusion is meant to be per-\(b_0\) (in which case the fix is to add a declared baseline index) or over all \(b\in B\) (in which case the fix is to exclude all pairs with \(a=1\)). The document never claims any of its own derivations have been tested by experiment, and I have not treated any as if they had.
