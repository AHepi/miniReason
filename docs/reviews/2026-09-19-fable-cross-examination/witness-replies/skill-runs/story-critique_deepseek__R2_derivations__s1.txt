## 2. Scene by scene

### Sequence A — fidelity, sameness, selection, surprise (D1–D4)

**Note 1 — D1's injectivity is real, load-bearing, and correctly used; the claim is still one step short of its title.** On the page: "let \((\tau,\sigma)\) be injective on \(C\)", conclusion "on \(\tau[C]\), the signature that its anchor subnetwork \(\lambda(k)\) has on \(C\), up to the port translation". The job of the hypothesis is to turn the pointwise equality of third coordinates into a bijection of the two triple-sets, and it does exactly that: without it \(\tau[C]\) is a quotient of \(C\) and the two sets have different sizes. Keep it. Two things the claim is not. First, (K) defines one forward kind for "two components \(j,j'\)" with "a bijection of their footprints", and D1 compares a component of \(E\) with a *subnetwork* of \(D\) across two different contracts; the body concedes this ("the anchor may be a subnetwork rather than a component"), so the title "Kind preservation" is a label carrying more than the content. Second, I check the baseline exclusion you might expect to bite — (K) requires coincidence only on \(C\setminus\{(1,b):b\in B\}\) — and it does **not** bite: agreement on all of \(\tau[C]\) implies agreement on the baseline-excluded part, and injectivity makes \(\tau[C]\setminus\{\text{baselines}\}=\tau[C\setminus\{\text{baselines}\}]\). A better version of the claim is the push-forward form: \(\mathrm{sig}_{\tau[C]}(k)\) is the image of \(\mathrm{sig}_C(\lambda(k))\) with no extra hypothesis, and injectivity is precisely what upgrades image to bijection. That version would also survive its citations (Note 2).

**Note 2 — the injectivity is dropped in all three places the result is used.** Part V: "By Derivation 1, (F1) entails that every component of \(E\) has the signature of its anchor subnetwork on the translated contract." D1's own summary: "It shows that fidelity on a contract preserves signature on that contract: a faithful component is of one forward kind, on \(C\), with its anchor subnetwork." D10: "then by Derivation 1 the persistence components are of one forward kind, on that contract, with the continuity subnetworks of things." Injectivity is not implied by (F1) or (F2): take two edits that act identically on \(D\) and on \(E\); both fidelity conditions hold, and the signature sets have different sizes. So each of the three sentences is stronger than what was proved. D10 is the worst of the three, because it draws a kind-claim out of a contract-relative signature fact and its anchor is a subnetwork — both shortfalls of Note 1 at once.

**Note 3 — D2's claim uses a hypothesis it does not use, and its hypothesis restates its definition.** The definition: "the anchored relations \(\operatorname{proj}_{V_k}\operatorname{Sol}_{\lambda(k)}(a,b)\) coincide, and the pulled-back answer profiles coincide". The claim assumes "(F1), (F2) and (A) on \(C\), have the same anchor map up to that bijection, and \(\Gamma\) corresponds to \(\Gamma'\)". The proof reads "(F1) for each gives the anchored relations as the component relations; the shared anchor map and the bijection give coincidence; (A) gives the profiles." Since the anchor map is *shared*, the coincidence of anchored relations follows from the shared anchors alone without (F1); and (F2) is never mentioned, so it is idle. The claim is a consistency remark on the definition, not a result, and it carries no job the following paragraph does not carry better ("Two candidates satisfying (F1), (F2), (A) on \(C\) with *different* decompositions, or different active commitments, are not one account"). If you want the claim to earn its place, make it say something the definition does not already say — that the relation is an equivalence, and how coarse it is relative to identity of organization — and check transitivity across different anchor maps, which is the only untrivial part. Also, the name is a label: nothing in it mentions (E)'s non-circularity, non-vacuity or adequacy, so two account-equivalent candidates can differ on whether they are accounts at all, and the title "Account-equivalence" implies otherwise.

**Note 4 — D9 is valid, weak and fine, and it is a second route to D2's job.** "If \(M_0,M_1\) have the same input–output projection and differ on an account claim, no function of the projection agrees with the claim on both", proved by "Equal inputs to a function give equal outputs." The proof is valid for the claim, and the choice of *projection* rather than answer profile is held by the three instances it names — "Parallel and priority wiring", "attribution from emitted text", "use inferred from delivery logs" — all projection-level. Nothing to fix. But it and D2's closing paragraph make one point at two levels. Per the two-routes rule, each must carry a second job: D9 carries the concrete instance and the two attributions that are ruled out; D2 carries the definition. Keep both, and say in one line that D9 is the general case and D2 the within-contract case, or a reader will count them as two results.

**Note 5 — D3's closure hypothesis is where all the content is, and the proof needs a hypothesis the claim does not state.** The proof's one load-bearing sentence is "Survival on \(H\) constrains only the relations at pairs in \(H\)." I check this: relations are indexed by the pair, so it is true — *provided* no off-\(H\) pair shares its image under \((\tau,\sigma)\) with an on-\(H\) pair. If \((a,b)\in C\setminus H\) and \((a',b')\in H\) have the same \((\tau(a),\sigma(b))\), the altered pair changes the relation *at the \(H\)-pair as well*, so it no longer "agrees with \((E,t)\) on \(H\)" and does not survive, and the promised member may not exist. So D3 needs "\((\tau,\sigma)\) injective on \(C\)" — the same clause as D1, here absent and needed for the claim, not merely the proof. Second, "the admissible class (the relations realizable by some member of \(\mathcal T\))" defines the class by the population that is being closed, and a relation realizable by a member *somewhere* is demanded *at this pair*; say per-slot or the hypothesis is unclear. Third, no example of a closed population is given in a document that is otherwise careful to say of (EK) "No instance of (EK) is asserted"; Part XV does the scoping ("A population *not* closed in that way ... does not refute anything"), so this is bookkeeping rather than substance. What works and should be protected: the Consequence paragraph — "It does not follow that the survivor *is* unfaithful there — it may be faithful on all of \(C\) — and nothing here says the survivor will fail" — holds the difference between *no guarantee* and *prediction of failure*, which is the distinction the whole Part 0 grievance 3 rests on. It also passes the flip test: had the population been parametric, the conclusion would reverse, and the same paragraph says so.

### Sequence B — questions, completeness, indices (D5–D7)

**Note 6 — D5's "exactly" fails in both directions, and it rests on a clause of Part III that is itself mis-stated.** The claim: "Its solutions are exactly the coherently posed questions on \(D\)." Coherent posing is defined in Part III as "\(\operatorname{Sol}_D(1,b_0)\neq\varnothing\), \(\mathcal Q\) is defined on every pair in \(C\), and \(S\) and \(K\) are disjoint from each other's complements as stated." (i) The construction enforces none of the first two: the component list has components about "in" ports, factor closure, and S/K consistency, and nothing that makes the target's baseline solution-set non-empty or makes \(\mathcal Q\) total on \(C\). (ii) The third clause, read literally, says \(S\subseteq K\) and \(K\subseteq S\), that is \(S=K\); since \(S\) is a subset of admitted pairs and \(K\)'s pairs are pairs "the module does not admit", the clause is satisfied only degenerately, and it contradicts the form \(C=(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\cap S)\cup K\) that Part III states two paragraphs earlier. It should read \(S\cap K=\varnothing\). D5 merely says "\(u_S\) and \(u_K\) are consistent with the 'in' ports in the form of Part III", so it inherits an unclear clause. (iii) The other direction: the components impose something Part III does not — "if \(u_{(a_2a_1,b)}\) reads 'in' then so do its factors where the composition is defined" — a factor-closure condition on contracts that no definition requires. (iv) The port set has "one port \(u_{(a,b)}\) for each \((a,b)\in A\times B\)" but no port for \(O_p\) or for \(\rho_p\), so no solution is a whole question tuple; \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\), which the required form uses, is neither a port nor in the boundary ("Boundary: the target \(D\) and \(b_0\), held fixed"); and (O)'s demand that \(A\) be "closed under a partial associative composition with identity \(1\)" is never discharged for the listed edits. Also "determines" is stronger than the construction, which fixes the ports and leaves the relations for "alter \(\mathcal Q\)", "alter \(S\)", "add a pair to \(K\) with a meaning" described in words rather than written. Better version if this is serious: either state the claim as "the solutions are the pair-patterns and query data the closure conditions admit, together with the fixed \(O_p\)" and say what that buys, or write the \(L_j(a,b)\), add the two missing coherence components, put \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) in the boundary, and drop or derive the factor closure.

**Note 7 — D5 has two routes to the contract, and they can disagree.** \(C\) is carried by the free "in" values of the \(u_{(a,b)}\) ports *and* derived as \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\cap S\) from the \(u_S\) component together with \(K\). Two routes to one job is fine only if each also does a second job, and here they can contradict: a valuation may set a pair "in" that lies in neither \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\cap S\) nor \(K\), or set a pair "out" that lies in \(S\). This is a case of an added detail undoing another's work, and it is the mechanism by which Note 6's solution set is larger than the questions. Fix: derive the value of each pair port from \(S\), \(K\) and \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) by a component, so the "in" pattern is not free.

**Note 8 — D5's stated limit is consistent with the rest of the document; leave it alone.** "What the construction cannot do is add a port to \(D\)" matches Part X's "the question-organization of Derivation 5 is built over an existing \(A\times B\)" and Part 0's attack (E), which concedes the same case. The merit condition cited here is collected in Part XV ("A question found that the merit condition wrongly rejects ..."). Planted and collected, nothing to fix.

**Note 9 — D6's "the only cycle that could arise" is false; I found two other routes.** The proof: "the only cycle that could arise — construction requiring a represented target while representation requires construction — is broken by Part IV, where a construction witness requires an *instantiated* target and (R) is not used inside any provenance witness." That part is right as far as it goes: Part IV's Constructed clause says "available as a target, in the module's sense of Inst, not in the sense of the representation predicate below", and I found no other use of (R) in a *witness* except the word "representation" in the blindness clause of Sel. But two other dependency routes loop, and both fall inside the Parts II–XIII range the claim covers.

- **Route one, the repertoire.** Deploy is defined by (R): "holds a representation of \(c\) — by (R)". The repertoire is "the set of contents deployable in some nontrivial use respect". Build's nontriviality clause uses it — "neither a relabelling of a binding already in the repertoire" — and New uses it again, "\(\operatorname{New}(s,c,h,e)\iff\neg\exists d\in R_{<e}(s,h),\ d\equiv_\ell c\)". So Build → repertoire → Deploy → (R) → Sel∨Con → "an episode (Part X) whose construction witness prepares \(t\)" → Build. Part XIV's parenthetical — "provenance depends on \(\Theta\) and on Part X's witnesses, which depend on \(\operatorname{Inst}\) and \(\equiv_\ell\) but not on (R)" — is contradicted by Part X's own text.
- **Route two, ownership and capability.** Part X: "A subhistory is **owned** by \(s\) when every process occurrence in it is an execution of a capability \(s\) possesses in the sense of Part XII (\(\operatorname{Can}\))". Part XII: "\(\operatorname{Can}_{\Omega,\beta}(\xi,T;\chi)\) requires an owned retained realization or an owned, physically admitted, finite construction of one". So Ownership → Can → owned → Ownership, and every construction witness needs "an owned subhistory of \(h\)".

Each is fixable in a line, and each fix also says which reading is intended: qualify the repertoire in Build's nontriviality clause as \(R_{<e}(s,h)\) (New already does, so this is likely the intent); give Can and Ownership as a greatest fixed point, as CT2 already does for retention, or add a descent condition on the capability invoked; replace "representation" in the blindness clause with the descriptive appositive the clause itself supplies ("a record pairing the state at which fidelity failed with what would have held"). ## 3. How it fits together

The document's own promise is to derive what can be derived and to mark the rest as declared, and Part XVI is where that promise is kept or broken. It is kept, with reasons I can point to, in D1's "What this does and does not show", in D3's Consequence, in D4's self-demotion, in D7's indexing, in Part XIII's question-begging clause (which deliberately uses a faithful transport rather than (R)), and in Part VII's "Adequacy is relative to the declared admission relation". Reading the derivations as a pattern count against that question — *what may a derivation carry?* — D1, D3, D4, D7 and D10 mark their limits; D2 is neutral; and D5, D6 and D8 do not, in exactly the three places the architecture stands on: question-finding as a content, the two-package partition, and equivariance, which underwrites \(\equiv_\ell\), hence recoding, newness, and the construction story. The fair mirror is unequal in one direction worth naming: Part XV demands of a refuter that it "fail none" of the named conditions and is exact about "under their stated assumptions", while D6 claims completeness of the definitional order with no witness at all and no place in the refutation list. One pair does click, and should be protected: Part 0's "two components no admitted change separates are one forward kind there, and the claim that they 'really' differ is either a claim about a finer contract, which is a new question, or a claim about their histories" against D10's closing "identity, at this grain and contract, is exhausted by trajectory."

## 4. The faults that matter most, ranked

1. **D6 (Notes 9–11).** The proof identifies one cycle and asserts it is the only one; two more routes close on the page (Build → repertoire → Deploy → (R) → Con → Build; Ownership → Can → owned → Ownership), Part XIV's parenthetical is contradicted by Part X, and six symbols used as inputs are in neither box, against Part 0's "they are all listed". This is first because Part XIV's list is what the whole document's economy rests on. All three parts are repairable in a handful of lines.
2. **D5 (Notes 6–7).** "Its solutions are exactly the coherently posed questions on \(D\)" fails in both directions, over a coherence clause that read literally says \(S=K\), with two of the three coherence conditions unenforced, no ports for \(O_p\) or \(\rho_p\), \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) outside the boundary, and two routes to \(C\) that can disagree. This is second because question-finding is one of the four items of the constitutive conjecture.
3. **Hypotheses stated but not carried (Notes 1–2, 5, 14, 16).** D1's injectivity is correct and is dropped in Part V, in its own summary, and in D10; D3 uses an injectivity it never states, in the claim itself and not only the proof; D8's proof uses a structure-preservation requirement its statement omits and its preservation list is thinner than the predicates it claims to preserve. One kind of fault in four places, and the cheapest to close.
