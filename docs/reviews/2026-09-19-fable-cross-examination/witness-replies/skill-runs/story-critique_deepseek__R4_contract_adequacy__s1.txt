# Contract adequacy as a conjunct of Account — critique

I read the document from the page (`20_semantics_revised`), Part V in full, and then every passage that leans on the condition: Part 0, Part III, Part VII, Part XI, Part XIV, Part XV, and Derivations 2, 3, 7, 8. The document is a closed formal object, so I have run the passes on its Parts as scenes, its conditions and witnesses as characters and choices, and its two grade-promises as the promises of its type. Where a test does not transfer I say so in one line.

## 1. Corrections first

**1. Your paraphrase drops the phrase that is under-specified.** You write the condition as "no admitted pair outside C at which the target's answer moves while the candidate's does not, or the reverse." Part V says: "there is no admitted pair \((a,b)\in C_{\mathrm{adm}}\setminus C\) at which \(\operatorname{Ans}_p\) differs from its baseline value while \(\operatorname{Ans}_E(\tau(a),\sigma(b))\) does not, or the reverse." The words "from its baseline value" are the whole of your first question, and they are not in your summary. Your diagnosis is right and under-states itself.

**2. You are right that it is a conjunct of (E), and the revision was not carried through the rest of the document.** Part V: "\(\operatorname{Account}(E,p,t,\Gamma)\iff \text{(F1)}\land\text{(F2)}\land\text{(A)}\land\text{NonCircular}\land\text{NonVacuous}\land\text{Adequate}\)." But Part 0, the section titled "Read this first", still defines the first grade as "a candidate whose components and assembled organization are faithful to their anchors under every change the contract admits, that answers the question asked, that does not restate the answer, and whose contract is stated." Four clauses; adequacy is not among them. So the document answers your question two ways: yes in Part V, no in Part 0. Part I's gloss, "Account at a contract is what a question can assess," has the same gap, and it is the gloss you are implicitly testing.

**3. Grievance 4 states the same condition more narrowly than Part V does, and the narrower version is wrong.** Grievance 4: "a contract is protective for a candidate when some **physically admitted** change it excludes would alter the target's answer and not the candidate's, or the reverse." But Part III says \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) is "for a physical organization, the physically admitted edits \(A_\Theta\) …; for a mathematical or modelled organization, a **declared admission relation** stated with the question." Part V says "admitted pair". Part VII's odd-order example says "Adequacy is relative to the declared admission relation." So Part V's word is the operative one and Grievance 4's gloss silently drops the mathematical case. Minor, but it means Grievance 4 cannot be used as a statement of the condition.

## 2. Scene by scene, grouped by sequence

### Sequence A — what the contract's form already does, and what only adequacy does

**4. Non-vacuity's exclusion half is entailed by the form of \(C\), so adequacy is the only condition in (E) that actually scrutinises an exclusion.** Non-vacuity says "every admitted pair excluded from \(C\) is excluded by the stated scope \(S\), not silently." But Part III already defines \(C=(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\cap S)\cup K\) and \(K\) as pairs "the module does not admit", so an admitted pair outside \(C\) is outside \(S\) by construction; the sentence cannot fail. *Job of adequacy:* it is the only conjunct that can fail on a stated exclusion. Remove it and the exclusion half of (E) becomes pure bookkeeping. **This makes it held, and tells the writer to protect it** — which is exactly why the evaluability gaps below are serious and not cosmetic.

### Sequence B — evaluability: your two well-definedness questions

**5. The candidate's baseline is not fixed anywhere.** "It contains the baseline \((1,b_0)\)" fixes the target's baseline. (F2) fixes "\(\tau(1)=1\)" and says nothing about \(\sigma\) at the baseline boundary; nor does Part IV, where "\(\sigma\) translates boundaries". So "\(\operatorname{Ans}_E(\tau(a),\sigma(b))\) does not [differ from its baseline value]" compares the candidate's value at \((\tau(a),\sigma(b))\) with a baseline the page never places. *Answer to your question "which baseline value": the page does not settle it.* The charitable reading — candidate baseline at \((1,\sigma(b_0))\), following (F2) — is self-consistent but unstated, and makes the two baselines sit at different boundaries unless \(\sigma(b_0)=b_0\), which is nowhere asserted. **A one-line fix: require the transport to carry the baseline pair to the baseline pair.**

**6. The query can be undefined at an excluded pair, for the same reason.** (Q) reads "\(\operatorname{Ans}_p(a,b)=\mathcal Q(D,a,b)\)." Coherent posing requires only that "\(\mathcal Q\) is defined on every pair in \(C\)" (Part III) — not on \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\). Adequacy evaluates \(\operatorname{Ans}_p\) at pairs in \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\setminus C\). So the first clause of the condition can be unevaluable on the *target* side, not only the candidate side. The page does not say which way that cuts either.

**7. The candidate's translation can be undefined at an excluded pair, and the page does not say which way that cuts — this is the load-bearing gap.** Part IV defines "\(\tau:A_D\to A_E\) translates edits" on "the stated scope". Part VII shows the case in the document's own example: "here \(\tau\) sends the intervention on \(H\) to the edit that replaces the calculation's component assigning \(H\), which the reversed organization has none of, so \(\tau\) is undefined and (F2) fails at that pair." So the document knows \(\tau\) can be undefined. Now apply adequacy's clause "\(\operatorname{Ans}_E(\tau(a),\sigma(b))\) does not [differ]": with \(\tau(a)\) undefined there is no value to compare, so the clause does not hold and no pair separates — the candidate is let off precisely where its translation cannot reach. Under the other convention — the one (F2) uses, where an undefined \(\tau\) makes the requirement fail — every excluded pair becomes separating for any candidate whose \(\tau\) is defined only on \(C\), which is the normal case. **These two readings give opposite verdicts on the document's own worked example, and nothing on the page chooses between them.** This is a hole, not a marked mystery: the document marks its deliberate gaps (Part XV, "What is not represented"), and this one is marked nowhere.

**8. The same gap sets Part V against Part VII.** Part V: "An account whose contract excludes an admitted change that would tell it apart from the target fails adequacy." Part VII: "The reversed calculation is faithful under the identification contract, whose edits alter the observed \(L\): it is an account at that contract." The identification contract excludes the production edits; the production edits tell the reversed calculation apart from the target ("intervening on \(H\) changes the target's \(L\) but not the calculation's \(H\)"). So under the strict reading these two sentences cannot both stand, and under the lenient reading the revision does not do the job Part V gives it. I take this as the *unquestioned first case* the lenses warn about: the first place in the document where a scope drawn to hide the separating change is endorsed, and nobody applies the condition to it.

**9. (E)'s rationale for having no provenance condition is not obviously preserved by adequacy.** "(E) carries **no provenance condition** … because a modeller must be able to assess an account without settling its history." Adequacy does not need a history, but it does need the module that supplies \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\). That is a smaller ask, but the sentence should say so, since it is the stated justification for the shape of (E).

### Sequence C — the question of force

**10. Literally, yes: it is too strong, in one precise sense.** If the target moves at any admitted excluded pair and the candidate does not, \(C\) is protective and the candidate fails. So a target with *any* out-of-scope dependence forces the candidate to reproduce that dependence's pattern of movement. For the document's own identification account this is decisive: Part VII says the reversed calculation is "not anchored as a production account" for lack of the production response — adequacy makes the same lack fail it at the *first* grade, which Part VII does not intend. Under the strict reading of note 7, therefore, the revision silently overrules Part VII.

**11. It is also too weak, in a way that survives every reading.** Both clauses of the condition turn on "differs from its baseline value", a binary. A pair at which both answers move, however differently, never fires. So the condition catches a candidate that goes **silent** off \(C\); it cannot catch a candidate that goes **wrong** off \(C\). Compare the anchored grade's condition 4: "fidelity holds not only on the pairs the contract selected but on every pair the module admits." That is the clause that catches the wrong-going candidate. **So adequacy and condition 4 are not rivals; they catch two different candidates, and the document never says which is for which.** If condition 4's word "fidelity" is meant to reinstate (F2) as well as (F1) on \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\), adequacy does no independent work at the anchored grade at all; if it is meant to reinstate only (F1), adequacy is the only clause that reaches the assembly off \(C\). Part V should settle that in one sentence, because it decides whether adequacy is a third condition or a duplicate.

**12. Part XV's open attack is not hypothetical; your question is its instance.** Part XV: "A protective contract the adequacy condition admits. A stated scope, passing Part V's adequacy condition, that nonetheless excludes exactly the admitted change that would separate the candidate from the target." Under the lenient reading of note 7, Part VII's identification account is a live instance. So the answer to "can a scope exclude exactly the separating change and still pass" is: **yes, through the undefined-translation route**, on the document's own example. That the document lists the attack honestly is to its credit; that its own example instantiates it is not.

**13. Swap test on the word "admitted".** Replace "admitted pair \((a,b)\in C_{\mathrm{adm}}\setminus C\)" with Grievance 4's "physically admitted". The odd-order example then has no admission relation to range over and adequacy is unevaluable there — a job fails. So Part V's word "admitted" is **held**, by the job "keep the mathematical account assessable at its declared admission relation", and Grievance 4's "physically" is **free** and merely loose.

### Sequence D — the collisions you asked about

**14. It collides with Derivation 7 and Part VIII through the index, not through the substance.** Derivation 7: "The assessment is indexed to \(C\); its claim keeps its identity and its truth value at that index (Part VIII)." Part VIII: "A claim indexed to a contract keeps its identity when a later theory changes the current contract; its truth value at its own index does not change." But adequacy ranges over \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\setminus C\), and \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\) is supplied by "the module in force" (Part III), which is the primitive package of Part XIV and appears nowhere in the index list — "the indices — grain \(\ell\) (constrained by admissibility), boundary \(\beta\), continuity \(\Omega\)." So a later theory that changes the module but leaves the contract set unchanged changes the adequacy verdict at the same contract, and Part VIII's fixity is broken. **Either the module joins the index, or adequacy cannot be a conjunct of a claim whose truth value is promised at \(C\).** Derivation 7's proof never mentions \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\), so as written it proves consistency for (F1), (F2) and (A) only.

**15. Derivation 7's catch-sentence over-reaches.** "The adequacy condition of Part V is what catches a change made to protect a candidate, whether or not it is recorded." Since \(K\cap C_{\mathrm{adm}}=\varnothing\), \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\setminus C = C_{\mathrm{adm}}\setminus S\), so adequacy ranges over the pairs the scope dropped. It can catch a **scope** change; it can never catch a change that goes through the module (moving a pair out of \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\)) or through \(K\) (a pair the module does not admit at all). Say "a change to the scope" and the sentence is true.

**16. It does not collide with the counterfactual set \(K\).** Part III settles it explicitly: "the objectivity condition of Part V ranges over \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\); a pair in \(K\) enters non-vacuity only through its stated meaning," and \(K\)'s members are by definition not admitted. Part VII already concedes the resulting limit for eliminative explanation ("what it establishes about the impossibility is relative to the stated meaning of the counterfactual"). **This test does not transfer to a fault; one line, moved on.**

**17. It does collide, in framing, with Grievance 4's two-index story.** Grievance 4 says "Two claims, two indices" and "An objectivity claim is the conjunction". That was true when Account was the first index and objectivity was the pair. Now Account *is* the pair, so "two indices" describes the conjuncts of one predicate rather than two claims, and the gloss should say so. No contradiction of substance, but a reader who takes Grievance 4 as the definition will assess the wrong object.

### Pattern count and the fair mirror

**18. The document meets one question six times and never makes two of the answers face each other.** The question: *when a claim's scope excludes a change its target answers to, what becomes of the claim?* Non-vacuity: state it. Adequacy: fail it. Anchored condition 4: fail the anchored grade. Derivation 2: "Underdetermination of an account by a contract is the semantics reporting that the contract does not contain the distinction. The remedies are a finer contract — a new question — or a history." Derivation 3: "selection provides no *guarantee* of fidelity where it did not test. It does not follow that the survivor *is* unfaithful there." Part VIII: fix the truth value at the index; start a new index for the change. Derivation 3 and adequacy are the same shape — fidelity on a tested subset, behaviour off it — and give opposite practical verdicts: *nothing follows* against *the candidate fails*. That is a fair-mirror fault, and it is checkable: put the two sentences side by side and say which object each governs. I suspect the answer is that Derivation 3 is about provenance and adequacy about scope, but the page does not say it, and a reader will supply the wrong one.

**19. No lucky meetings, no stock device — this test does not transfer.** Every junction in the document is a stated definition or a declared parameter; nothing arrives by convenience. Conversely the condition is not a stock note of the "raise the stakes" kind: it is stated as a condition on supplied relations, which is the document's own promise.

**20. Medium check: does not transfer.** A formal document has no camera and no inner head. The nearest analogue is the promise that every condition be evaluable from its stated inputs, and that is Notes 5–7.

## 3. How it fits together

The condition is genuinely load-bearing and the writer should keep it: it is the only conjunct of (E) that can fail on a stated exclusion, and Part XI leans on it directly — "A later narrowing of the contract to rescue adequacy is a new claim at a new index, and does not retroactively satisfy (EK)." That is its second job, and it is a good one. What does not fit is the company it now keeps. It reaches outside \(C\) into \(C_{\mathrm{adm}}\), which makes the first grade no longer "what can be assessed within a question"; it overlaps condition 4 without saying which catches which candidate; it is absent from Part 0's definition of the very predicate it now helps to define; and it puts the module, which is not on the index list, into the truth conditions of a claim whose fixity is promised at \(C\). The case you are attacking — the reversed calculation at the identification contract — sits exactly where these four seams meet.

## 4. The faults that matter most, ranked

**First: the undefined pair decides the condition's force, and nothing on the page chooses.** \(\tau\) can be undefined (Part VII), \(\mathcal Q\) need only be defined on \(C\) (Part III), and \(\sigma(b_0)=b_0\) is nowhere asserted. Fix: one sentence for each — "a pair at which the candidate's translation is undefined is a separating pair" (or the reverse, stated), and the baseline requirement — and then re-read Part VII's "it is an account at that contract" against it. This is the fix that settles your "too strong or too weak" question, because it *is* that question.

**Second: adequacy and anchored condition 4 are doing one job with two rules and no division of labour.** State in Part V whether condition 4's "fidelity … on every pair the module admits" reinstates (F2) as well as (F1). If it does, adequacy's only content is the binary silent/not-silent test and it should be described as such, not as the objectivity conjunct. If it does not, adequacy carries the assembly off \(C\) and Part V should say so — and then the two-grade story in Part 0 needs the correction in Note 2.

**Third: the module is now part of the index, and the document has not recorded it.** Amend Part VIII and Derivation 7, or move adequacy out of (E) and back to being the objectivity conjunct of Grievance 4. Those are the two real options; the current text takes the first without paying for it.
