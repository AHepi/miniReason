## The strongest criticism of my previous response

The previous response found four of six memo claims defective (B1, B2, B3, B5) and two sound (B4, B6), and its most detailed positive contribution was an attributed divergence between contract R4 and WHL's own `active_hold`/`reserves_effect` pair at the boundary t = expiry. That divergence claim is stated in a way that conflates two different disagreements, and the conflation makes the contract appear to "pick a side" between two specimen definitions when in fact the specimen's two definitions do not disagree with each other at all, and the real disagreement is between the contract and *both* of them.

Look at what the previous response says. Under B3 it writes: "WHL's `active_hold` uses `¬ (t > r.expiry)` and `reserves_effect` uses `if t > r.expiry then 0 else r.quantity` — note these are *not* the same test: `¬(t > e)` is `t ≤ e`, whereas `reserves_effect` zeroes when `t > e`, i.e., `t ≥ e+1`. So on the natural numbers the specimen's own `active_hold` and `reserves_effect` disagree at `t = expiry` (active_hold says active; reserves_effect also returns the quantity there, since `t > e` is false)."

That last parenthetical is the problem. It asserts the two definitions "disagree at t = expiry," then supplies a clause showing they agree there: `reserves_effect` returns the quantity at t = e because `t > e` is false, which is the same verdict `active_hold` gives via `¬(t > e)`. Two definitions that both classify t = expiry as active do not disagree. The previous response manufactured a disagreement inside the specimen that the specimen does not contain. Its own parenthetical is a self-refutation, and it prefaces the manufactured disagreement with "note these are not the same test" — but "not the same syntactic test" is not "not the same verdict." `¬(t > e)` and `(t > e)` are literal complements, so on the active/expired partition they are the *same* test up to negation; they classify every t identically. The previous response treated a syntactic contrast as a semantic divergence.

This matters for more than tidiness. The previous response then builds a three-way picture — contract, `active_hold`, `reserves_effect` — in which the contract "resolves the boundary" in favor of equality-is-zero and the specimen is split on the question. The correct picture is simpler and more consequential: the specimen is internally consistent at the boundary (both definitions treat t = expiry as active, reserving the quantity), and the entire boundary disagreement is between contract R4 and the specimen as a whole. R4 says strictly `t < expiry` reserves, so at equality the hold reserves zero; the specimen reserves the quantity through and including the expiry instant. That is one clean divergence, not a specimen-internal split with the contract adjudicating. Had a later cycle tried to invoke "the specimen's own two definitions disagree" as support for either the contract's reading or the specimen's reading, it would have been invoking a premise this response invented. I am withdrawing that premise and restating the divergence as contract-versus-specimen.

## A second, smaller defect in the previous response

Under B3 the previous response also says the contract is "stricter than the specimen's `reserves_effect` in the opposite direction: R4 says at equality the hold reserves zero, which is the deletion-side behavior at the boundary." The "deletion-side" phrasing imports RSS's `expired_deletes_rule` vocabulary, but contract R4 is not a deletion rule: R4 keeps the record's revision authority and changes only its effect ("Expiry changes a retained record's effect, not its revision authority"). Calling equality-is-zero "deletion-side" is a representational slip that could suggest R4 deletes the record at expiry, which it does not. The correction: R4's boundary behavior is a reservation-effect choice, not a deletion. This is minor but worth flagging because it is the same family of error as the first — using one resource's vocabulary (RSS's deletion reading) to characterize a rule in another resource (the contract), where the contract explicitly separates retention from effect.
