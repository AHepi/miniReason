# Cycle 11 answer: Memo C as a target, and the limits of the representation's own commitments

So both WHL-as-used and my own prior cycles' appraisal method are legitimate objects of the memo; a defect in a clause of Memo C is not automatically a defect in the memo's usefulness, and a defect in a language clause is not automatically a defect in every application of that language.

The method is the selected policy. I convert each of C1–C6 to a WHL-shaped claim where that is honest, check it against R1–R8 and against what my own earlier cycles actually said and did, and then render back to prose. I distinguish:

## C1 — "rejection of a supporting assumption is a refutation; therefore A's conclusion is established false for every account"

**C1 as stated is defective. It overstates the consequence and mislabels the operation.**

The correct relation, which R7 states directly, is: a criticism can target a rule, data, an interpretation, an earlier criticism, or a representation; and rejection of one supporting assumption *defeats that support route without proving its conclusion false*. So rejection of an assumption is not "a refutation" in the RSS sense of (c), and even RSS's sense (c) — "a demonstration that a conjecture depends on an assumption that another account rejects" — is explicitly *local to accounts and does not force global agreement*. Memo C says "for every account." That is the specific overreach.

Under WHL: a `Refutation c` is a `(snap, ¬ c snap)` pair — a counterexample, not a dependency claim. The vocabulary has no notion of "assumption" at all (the notes say so explicitly: "source assumptions are not primitive"). So C1's operation is not even expressible as a refutation in WHL's own terms, and the memo's "therefore" is not whatever the contract calls refutation.

**Where C1 has a point.** The memo's underlying worry — that calling an assumption-rejection a "refutation" in the ordinary vocabulary invites overreading — is real, and my own prior answers contributed to that vocabulary: cycle 6's "consequential new problem" section refers to "Memo A repeatedly substitutes alternative or unsupplied readings," and earlier cycles label A2/A4/A6 "defective." So the memo is right that a critic's corrective vocabulary can slide from "defeat this support" to "establish falsity." What the memo gets wrong is converting that risk into a universal-consequence rule. The right restatement: an assumption-rejection defeats one support route; it does not establish the conclusion false for any account, much less all.

Attribution: the fix is to keep the label "defective" scoped to a specific clause with a stated fault line (as cycles 1 and 6 tried to do), not to raise refutation to a global status. My earlier wording is the practice Memo C is criticizing; I do not withdraw the earlier verdicts, but I endorse reading their scope as clause-local, which is what they mostly said.

## C2 — "commitment anywhere settles inquiry globally concerning that conjecture"

**Defective as stated.** This directly contradicts R8's representation of uncertainty, retention, withdrawal, and suspension as legitimate, and it contradicts RSS's scheme where commitment is *local to an account* and different accounts may commit to incompatible conjectures while unresolved questions persist as *open questions* in inquiry states.

Under WHL: a `Commitment : Prop` is endorsed by whoever endorses it; WHL explicitly says it "does not assert which conjectures are true; it provides the vocabulary to state them." There is no primitive for "commitment status is now global." So C2's inference is not supported by WHL, and the contract R7 distinguishes "what is proposed or disputed" from "what is settled."

The useful kernel inside C2: *if* an account commits to C, then within that account, questions the account treated as open under C are no longer open *for that account*. That is local and is closer to RSS's account-local commitment. But C2 states it as "no unresolved question concerning that conjecture can remain anywhere," which is this inquiry's environment exactly refusing. The two prior cycles retained open questions (the cross-booking coupling question carried forward since cycle 5's B6 treatment; the exact-set-versus-bounds gap as an open question until cycle 10 closed it against the two supplied snapshots) — so the memo's own background environment is a counterexample to its global-settlement claim.

## C3 — "every projection must lose information"

**Defective as a universal; sound only under a stated restriction, which C3 itself names and then does not honour.**

C3's two rider clauses ("this also holds on a singleton input domain and when the projection is injective over the admitted domain") are the substantive repair. On a singleton domain, no information is lost by projecting — the projections of the singleton coincide with the element. On an injective projection over the admitted domain, information loss is reversible. Memo C asserts universality and then names exactly the cases where it fails. That is not a scope caveat; it is a self-undermining universal.

Under WHL: `reserves_effect` is a function, and on the restriction to held records with a fixed expiry policy, on a single-record bag, its value determines the input's quantity. So "projection must lose information" is false in WHL's own sample function. The RSS clause C3 echoes — "a projection is always a loss of information" — is stated in RSS as one of the *unresolved expressive limitations* of the class, i.e. as an RSS-internal claim, not as a semantic theorem; and even RSS offers it about the *availability projection* from a set of reservations to a single number, not about all functions.

So the usable version of C3 is: *when the projection's target is strictly coarser than the admitted domain — different admitted elements map to the same output — information loss is unavoidable for distinguishing those elements; otherwise it is not.* This is the same distinction cycle 10 already used when it kept the *set* {1, 5} distinct from the *bounds* [1, 5]. The set is a projection of the combination space that still distinguishes the two combinations; the interval does not. C3 is right about the interval-like case and wrong about all cases.

## C4 — "no conflict-resolution algorithm supplied ⟹ the problem is noncomputable"

**Defective.** This conflates "the semantics does not pick a reading" with "no algorithm exists for any specified subproblem under a fixed reading."

RSS says its noncomputability is *deliberate*: it "does not provide an algorithm for resolving conflicts" and "treats any demand for a single answer as a projection that must be justified outside the semantics." That is a design decision about the *semantic class*, not a proof that the underlying problems are undecidable. Under WHL and R6, once the reading is fixed (as R3–R5 fix it for this task), the exact availability set is *computable*: enumerate the finite per-booking alternative sets, take the Cartesian product, compute capacity minus the sum. Cycle 10 did exactly this for cases A and B; the computation is finite and mechanical.

So C4's inference is invalid. What RSS's noncomputability shows is that RSS refuses to commit to a single reading, not that no reading yields a computable function. The correct statement is: *this semantic class does not choose a reading, so an external choice is needed before any computation can begin.* That is weaker and is in fact what RSS says. "Noncomputable" attaches to the semantics-as-unified-choice-machine, not to the underlying reservation and availability functions.

One point in C4's favour that I will not silently drop: *if* the semantics is required to produce a single answer without any supplied reading, then no algorithm follows *from the semantics alone*, because the reading is not in the semantics. But that is a claim about the semantics' output policy, not about mathematical noncomputability of the reserved problem, which is what C4 asserts.

## C5 — "a Lean predicate's existence proves the name captures the source prose and that application is adequate"

**Defective, and it is a defect in *method*, not in WHL.** This is the item that most directly targets my own practice.

A predicate *states* a relation; it does not prove that the relation is the intended one for the source prose. R1 forbids reading the label as evidence of coverage; R7 distinguishes what a source *states* from what follows. WHL's own notes are explicit and self-critical on exactly this point: WHL declares multiple expressive limitations (no replication primitive, no delivery provenance, no cross-booking resource model, no assumption-dependence, no per-replica logical time), and it says the language "does not choose between" the standard reservation reading and `expired_deletes_rule`. So the specimen *itself* documents that the presence of a named predicate does not certify coverage.

The two places where this has mattered in this inquiry, named without over-claiming causation:

- **Expiry boundary.** WHL's `active_hold` names `¬ (t > expiry)`, i.e. `t ≤ expiry`, which contradicts the contract's R4 strict `t < expiry`. The predicate's name and existence tell you nothing about which boundary is correct; the contract R4 decides, and cycles 5, 6, 9, 10 attributed the fixing authority to R4, not to the predicate name. This is C5's point *in action* inside the supplied specimen: the existence of `active_hold` did not make it semantically adequate to the task.
- **`exact_duplicate` and provenance.** Cycle 6 noted that `exact_duplicate` is a five-field test with no provenance field, and that its use as evidence about duplicate-carrying of authority is analogy, not definitional fit. That is the right discipline under C5: the predicate's existence certified nothing about provenance, and the cycle-6 answer said so.

C5's positive kernel: a predicate is a *usable object of inquiry* whose name is a hypothesis, not a certificate. Its application being "adequate" requires checking against the source and the contract. That is the correct method, and it is what the selected policy's "check the conversion against the actual source claim" instruction already requires.

## C6 — "being able to express a false claim is not a defect by itself; the question is what distinctions the representation allows us to inspect, criticize, and use"

**Sound, and it is the most useful sentence in Memo C.** It is also textually faithful to WHL's own self-description: the specimen says it "does not assert which conjectures are true; it provides the vocabulary to state them and to record refutations." The function of a representation is to make distinctions inspectable, not to be infallible. RSS separately treats "permission to represent uncertainty without forcing resolution" as a designed feature.

The one thing C6 leaves open is *which* distinctions a representation must admit for a given task, and that is properly a scope question rather than a defect-or-not. WHL's declared limitations (replication, provence-as-authority, cross-booking resource, per-replica time) are the honest negative side of the same coin, and cycle 10 already gave that side its content when it listed, for the coupling question, the resources and methodological conditions that would have to be supplied to investigate it *without manufacturing a policy*.

## Where the memo is not wrong but raises open scope questions

- **C1's underlying observation** — that assumption-rejection and refutation are different operations — is correct as an observation, and the fix is not to broaden rejection into proof of falsity but to state it as defeating one support route. Whether that operation should get its own primitive (rather than living in prose, as R7 currently places it) is a scope question WHL already answers "no primitive" to.
- **C3's interior cases** (singleton, injective) are true, and the question is which side of the dichotomy a given projection falls on. Availability as a set versus as a bound interval is already an instance: cycle 10 showed both cases A and B fall on the lossy side of *bounds*, while the *set* projection retains the two distinct combinations.
- **C4's design point** — that RSS declines to choose a reading — is true and is the honest description of the class. The scope question it opens, whether any *external* reading-selection policy should be admissible, is exactly the open cross-booking coupling question that has been carried forward since cycle 5 and confirmed for case B in cycle 10. Memo C does not answer it and does not pretend to.
- **C5's methodological point** about predicate names is settled sound; the open question is what an application's adequacy criterion is, and that is supplied by the contract R1–R8 plus the source prose, not by the predicate.
- **C6's residual scope question** is already named above: which distinctions the representation must admit for a task. No new problem issues from it.

## Is there a consequential new problem in this cycle?

The task asks me to identify one only if the inquiry supplies grounds. I judge it does supply **one, and it is a scope problem in *my own prior cycles' appraisal vocabulary*, not in WHL and not in Memo C-as-a-whole.**

**The problem: my earlier cycles labelled Memo A and Memo B items "defective" without a stated axis of defect, so that later readers could not easily distinguish (i) a defect in the *representation's clause* from (ii) a defect in a *substituted reading* from (iii) a defect in *a use of the representation* on a particular snapshot from (iv) an *open scope question*, all four of which I produced under the single word "defective."**

Concretely, from the earlier answers that this cycle's re-presentation makes available:

- Cycle 1 calls A1 "defective" and also says A1's alternative reading is "statable in WHL" — that is a defect of *type (ii)*: a substitution of a rival reading for the fixed one, not a defect in a WHL clause or in the data.
- Cycle 1 calls A2 "defective" for equating interval with exact set — also type (ii) (a reading), sitting next to R6's *rule* about when bounds may be equated.
- Cycle 6 calls B1 "a defect" and "of the same family as A4's" — type (ii) again (provenance inflated into authority).
- Cycle 6 calls B2 "defective" for substituting display for delivery — type (iii) use-of-representation, since `Snapshot.records` and a display function are distinct slots.
- Cycle 6 records the "display-vs-bag" interface as *an open scope question about WHL* — type (iv) — while also calling the underlying inference defective.

All four are legitimate verdicts, but they are not *the same kind of verdict*, and the earlier cycles used one word for all four. Memo C then *reads its own way out of the word* in C1 and *overreads the practice* in C5. The consequential problem is not that any individual earlier verdict was wrong; it is that the earlier vocabulary does not let a later cycle or a reader tell C1-, C3-, C4-style overreads from the sound course when the verdict-word is reused.

The corrective the inquiry itself supplies, without manufacturing new apparatus: borrow the *partitions* already present in the sources rather than inventing one. R7 named the partition (statement / observation / assumption-relative consequence / proposal-or-dispute, plus rule-vs-data-vs-interpretation-vs-criticism-vs-representation as criticism targets). RSS named the partition (account-local commitment, account-local refutation of three kinds, open questions in an inquiry state). WHL named the partition (rule-conjecture vs data-conjecture, with the specimen's own declared limitations named one by one). So the working account's relabeling below uses R7's axes explicitly.

I name this as the *single* new problem because it is the one the inquiry actually supplies: the pattern across C1, C3, C4 and across cycles 1, 6 is that *the axis of defect went unstated*. Naming the axis is enough; it does not require a new language, only a discipline on the appraisal vocabulary, which R7 already prescribed.

## Usable working account

Stated in WHL-convertible form, then rendered to prose, and scoped to what the contract actually fixes:

1. **Assumption-rejection versus refutation.** Under R7, criticism of a rule, data, interpretation, earlier criticism, or representation defeats that route of support; it does not establish the conclusion false for any account, much less all. Under WHL, `Refutation c` requires a `(snap, ¬ c snap)` pair, not a dependency claim. C1 is therefore rejected in its universal form; the underlying observation that assumption-rejection and refutation differ is retained, along with the discipline of labelling a verdict "defective" only with its axis (see §7).

2. **Commitment is account-local.** Under R7 and RSS (the latter as comparison, not as the selected representation), a commitment settles a conjecture *for the account that makes it*; open questions persist across accounts and within inquiry states. C2 as stated is rejected. Unresolved questions carried by this inquiry — the cross-booking coupling question, the exact-set-vs-bounds question until cycle 10 closed it for the two supplied snapshots — are counterexamples to global settlement.
