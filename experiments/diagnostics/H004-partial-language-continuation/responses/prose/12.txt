## The most consequential defect: C3 was answered under the wrong criterion

C3 says "every projection must lose information. This also holds on a singleton input domain and when the projection is injective over the admitted domain."

My cycle-11 response calls C3 defective, on the ground that a projection can be lossless on a singleton domain or an injective projection over the admitted domain, so "must" fails. It then assimilates C3 to the cycle-9 B5 error about bounds-vs-exact-set and says the residual truth is "projections need not be lossless, and the projections actually used in the debate have not been shown to be lossless," which is "much weaker than C3 as written."

The criticism is warranted and it is not a minor refinement — it is a criterion error.

RSS explicitly defines "projection" as a function from a viewpoint and a reading to a *single answer* (for example, a single availability number), and says: "Projections are allowed but not privileged. A projection is always a loss of information." That last sentence is a stipulative claim of RSS as a semantic class, not a theorem my prior response can refute by counterexample. C3, read against RSS, is the *consequence of the definition*: if a projection collapses a viewpoint-and-reading (which is where the admitted alternatives live) to a single answer, then — under the criterion of "loss of information" that RSS uses, namely "the answer does not recover the admitted alternatives" — the answer does not recover them. In that stipulated sense, the singleton-domain case is not a counterexample at all, because the admitted domain for the projection in RSS is not the single output value; it is the alternative set the viewpoint admits.

So my cycle-11 response (a) read C3 as a *substantive* universal claim and refuted it by counterexample, while the more charitable and better-grounded reading is that C3 is *definitional under RSS's use of "projection"* — and (b) did not consider that reading explicitly enough to adjudicate. It gestured at the definitional reading in a scope note ("if 'projection' is stipulated by RSS to mean a map that is not injective on the admitted domain, then C3 (restricted to nothing) would be a definitional claim") but then dismissed the restriction as "not stipulated" by RSS. This is precisely the wrong move: RSS *does* stipulate the sense — "projection" is defined as a map to a single answer *from a viewpoint and a reading*, and the "loss of information" clause is attached to the motivation for the definition, not offered as an empirical generalization about candidate maps.

The consequence: my prior response overstated the strength of its own C3 criticism. The correct verdict on C3 is not "defective universal claim, defeated by singleton and injective cases." It is: *As a stipulative claim about projections in RSS's sense, C3 is sound in purpose but misstated in C3's own quantifier ("must... also holds on... when the projection is injective over the admitted domain").* Where C3 is defective is narrower than I said: the *universal quantifier over arbitrary functions* is not supported; the *claim about the specific notion of projection RSS defines* is not the thing C3's wording targets. So the memo and my prior response are each partly right about different readings of C3, and my prior response should have separated the two readings cleanly and declined to refute the definitional one by counterexample.

This matters because C3 is the memo's gateway to C6. If C3's correct reading is definitional, C6's function — "expressing a false claim is not a defect by itself" — is doing less work than my prior response assigned to it, and the "localizer becomes universalizer" diagnosis (the new problem I claimed) applies to C3 only under the substantive reading of C3, not under the definitional one.

## Second defect: C5's grounds were partly right, but the citation target is unstable

C5 says "a Lean predicate's existence proves that its name faithfully captures the source prose and that its application is adequate."

My cycle-11 response calls C5 defective, citing WHL's disclaimers ("declares a language, not a translation of any particular corpus"; "the language does not assert that any conjecture is true") and the *contract-vs-WHL* divergence at t = expiry between R4 and `reserves_effect`.

The verdict against C5 is right, but the citation of R4 vs `reserves_effect` at t = expiry is a target error in the appraising practice. R4 is the *declared contract*. WHL is a *fallible representation resource*. The passage in the prior response calls this "a divergence" and treats it as evidence that `reserves_effect` is not a faithful formalization of *the contract*. But the contract-spec R4 is not WHL's target prose; WHL says it "declares a language, not a translation of any particular corpus." So the correct statement is: R4 and `reserves_effect` are *two distinct readings*, one at strict-inequality expiry ("strictly less than expiry" in R4, `t > expiry` in the Lean code — which, at the semantic level where `reserves_effect` is compared with R4, do not agree at t = expiry). They are not a fact in which one "faithfully" formalizes and the other does not. My prior response got the verdict right (C5 defective) but the ground partly wrong: it cited a divergence as if it established that the Lean predicate is the unfaithful one, when the point is only that the two readings differ and neither is privileged by WHL itself.

The consequence: the prior response's use of the t = expiry case as evidence *against a Lean predicate's naming* overloads WHL. The cleaner ground for rejecting C5 is exactly WHL's own disclaimers, which the prior response also cites. Citing the divergence as additional evidence is a scope error of the same shape the prior response attributes to Memo C — a local fact about two readings inflated into evidence about a formalization's faithfulness.
