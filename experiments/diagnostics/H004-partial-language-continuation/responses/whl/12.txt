## 1. The single most important criticism of the cycle-11 response: it treated each memo-C clause as if it were one uniform kind of object

Across C1–C5, the cycle-11 response produced a characteristic shape: "the rule half is X; the consequence half is Y; therefore the claim is defective (or half-sound or mis-scoped)." That shape is *sometimes* the right shape. But it is applied even where the memo clause is doing a different job.

Concretely, C1 is written as a mini-argument: a premise ("our representation calls rejection of a supporting assumption a refutation") plus a consequence ("therefore … A's conclusion has been established false for every account"). Criticizing that as a two-part rule misnamed as a universal falsity (as cycle-11 did) treats the clause as if it were a proposed appraisal rule *endorsed* by someone. But the memo clause is *stated*, not endorsed. Memo C's own header says it "challenges a method and the representation's own commitments" and it is labeled "fallible prose." R7 already tells us reports, reconstructions and criticisms are fallible claims. So when I "reject" C1, I am not doing anything the memo has not already conceded about itself.

That is not fatal to the cycle-11 verdict, but it changes the *scope*. The verdict should have been: the clause misdescribes what the representation (WHL or RSS) actually does with assumption-rejection; the misdescription is memo C's, not the representation's; the correct description is already in R7 and in RSS's locality clause; and therefore C1 is a clause-level defect *in memo C* which, when read as a criticism of the representation, targets nothing the representation asserts. The cycle-11 verdict said roughly this, but the criticism I want to make is about the *method*: cycle-11 spent the first half of its C1 section establishing a two-part structure (rule half, consequence half) as if the clause's own structure were the issue. It is not. The issue is whether the clause *matches* the representation. The two-part structure was scaffolding that obscured the actual target.

**Consequence.** This is a defect of appraisal method, not of the covered language. It matters because memo C explicitly makes my appraisal practice a target this cycle, and the pattern (decompose the clause, name the parts, declare mis-scope) recurs in every section and is doing different work each time while pretending to be the same move.

## 2. C1 — a narrower criticism of the cycle-11 treatment, on the *selected* representation

The cycle-11 response correctly noted:

> "WHL does not call assumption-rejection a refutation; it does not encode assumption dependence at all."

That is accurate as far as WHL's *type declarations* go. `Refutation c` is `(snap : Snapshot) (not_c : ¬ c snap)`. The "Notes on intended meaning" say assumptions are not primitive.

But cycle-11 then concluded the clause "has no basis under WHL." That last step is too fast, and this is a real defect I want to *retain from my own earlier move and then widen*.

WHL does contain, as a *derived* relation, the notion of a refutation that targets a *conjecture about a rule* — `rules_disagree r1 r2 snap : Prop`, and `Refutation` is parametric in `Conjecture : Type := Snapshot → Prop`. So a conjecture can be about a rule (encoded as a snapshot-level claim about rule outputs). The file's own note says: "A refutation targets a conjecture by exhibiting a snapshot for which it fails." That is precisely the typed, snapshotted version of "defeat of the conclusion on this snapshot."

What WHL cannot do — and where cycle-11 was right — is express *support-route dependence*. You cannot in WHL write "conjecture J was reachable only under assumption R5." The file says this explicitly: "it does not encode a notion of assumption dependence." So a rejection of an assumption is *not* typed in WHL as a refutation. Fine.

But this means C1's target is not "WHL says rejection of an assumption is a refutation." C1's target is some *appraisal practice* (perhaps held by the memo's author, perhaps attributed to earlier cycles) that conflates the two. And on the WHL side, the accurate statement is stronger than cycle-11 made it: *WHL's expressive limitation is precisely the absence of the target C1 is criticizing*. The clause C1 rejects ("rejection of a supporting assumption [is] a refutation") is one *WHL cannot even state*, in either direction. So C1 is not a criticism of WHL; it is a remark that WHL is silent on a distinction the memo is treating as if WHL took a side on it.

**Retention.** Cycle-11's C1 verdict stands. **New criticism.** Cycle-11 could have said this more directly: WHL's *silence* on assumption dependence is not WHL *making* the conflation C1 attacks. That is a *missing distinction* in the representation (cycle-11's task scope explicitly says WHL cannot encode it), not an *endorsement* of the conflation.

## 3. C2 — the cycle-11 response is correct, and I do not retract it; a sharper formulation is available

Cycle-11 said C2 is a strawman: neither WHL nor RSS asserts that one account's commitment closes the question globally, and R7 contradicts it.

That is sound. RSS's inquiry state is "a collection of accounts plus a set of open questions"; a conjecture is "open if no account has committed to it or refuted it." One account's commitment removes *that account* from the open-question set for the conjecture; it does not close the question for any other account. WHL's `Commitment : Type := Prop` carries no globalising machinery. R7: rejecting a supporting assumption "can defeat that support route without proving its conclusion false."

**Sharper formulation of the same fact.** WHL and RSS are actually *aligned* here, by very different routes. RSS aligns by designing the inquiry state to hold multiple accounts and by making commitment local. WHL aligns by having *no* inquiry-state primitive at all — WHL cannot represent "global inquiry" as a state that a local commitment could settle, because the whole inquiry apparatus is on the memo side, not the representation side. So the *defect* in C2 is that C2 treats "the representation" as having a rule to be criticized. Neither selected text does.

I do not retract this verdict. I record it as a *retained commitment* from cycle 11.

## 4. C3 — the cycle-11 response is right in result, but its treatment of the target is off, and this is where I find the more interesting defect

Cycle-11's C3 verdict was: "half-sound, half-defective, and mis-targeted." The mis-targeting point is: "projections are always a loss of information" is RSS's slogan, not WHL's. Cycle-11 correctly quoted RSS: a projection is "a function from a viewpoint and a reading to a single answer… always a loss of information."

Now the criticism. Said exactly, cycle-11 was right that RSS's slogan overstates. But cycle-11 then did something the memo itself warns against. Memo C's clause C3 is *itself* not an endorsement; it is a claim attributed to "the representation," and cycle-11's response treated RSS as if RSS's slogan were a *load-bearing clause* of RSS, then said it isn't. That is a *rhetorical* criticism: it concedes the slogan exists, points at the slogan's overbreadth, and calls the clause "rhetorically overbroad and not load-bearing."

The problem is: on what basis is it "not load-bearing"? RSS's document says projections are always a loss of information; it does not add a footnote distinguishing injective projections. To *demonstrate* the slogan is not load-bearing in the sense that matters, we need a case where RSS's own semantics *needs* projections to be non-lossy. Cycle-11 named the correct phenomenon — R6's bound-versus-exact discipline, with the concrete instances {3, −2} vs [−2, 3] and {5, 1} vs [1, 5] — but it did not connect the phenomenon to the reason *RSS's slogan should be read as metaphorical*.

Let me supply the connection now, since I am criticizing the gap.

RSS's slogan claims that a projection to a single answer always loses information. But R6 in the *selected* contract is the opposite discipline: it allows the reporting of the *exact set* of admissible values, and R6 explicitly says "an interval may be reported as bounds but must not be equated with an exact set unless all intervening admitted integer values occur." That clause exists precisely because a projection to an interval *is* lossy in a specific sense (it drops the fact that interior values may be unattained), while a projection to the *exact set* need not be lossy on a given domain. So R6 encodes a *typed* distinction between lossy and non-lossy reports, whereas RSS's slogan collapses both cases into "always lossy."

The connection matters because it fixes the *why* of the mis-targeting: RSS's slogan is not merely "overbroad rhetoric"; it lacks the distinction R6 encodes, and that lack is *already* one of the reasons the selected policy is WHL and not RSS. Stating this more directly is a genuine improvement, not a new invention.

**Retention.** Cycle-11's "half-sound, half-defective, mis-targeted" verdict is sound in substance. **Improvement.** The *grounds* for "mis-targeted" should have been R6's typed discipline, not an appeal to RSS's tone.

## 5. C4 — the cycle-11 response is correct, and I add the *scope* the response left implicit

Cycle-11 said the clause "no algorithm supplied ⟹ noncomputability" is a category error; RSS's own declaration of noncomputability is a *stance*, not a theorem; and WHL's per-booking finite enumeration is decidable-by-construction over its admitted domain.

That is right. Two scoping points the cycle-11 text left implicit and that I now make explicit:

(1) The category error is not symmetric. "Noncomputable" is a property of functions (no TM computes them for all inputs); "no algorithm is supplied" is a property of a *presentation*. It is possible to have a computable underlying function represented without an algorithm — trivially, most math textbooks do this. So "no algorithm supplied" *and* "function is computable" are compatible; "no algorithm supplied" *and* "function is noncomputable" are also compatible. The clause's inference selects one of these two compatible cases and asserts it as the only one. That is not just a category error; it is a *non sequitur with an unmentioned alternative*. Cycle-11 said "category error"; the sharper diagnosis is "unwarranted disjunction collapse."

(2) On the *selected* representation, this does not matter to the actual task. WHL's task-scope R6 asks for "the exact set of possible availability values" when the task asks for it — that is an enumeration over finite greatest-revision sets. The computability question never arises under WHL because WHL's domain is finite per booking. So even if C4's conclusion were true as a claim about a general warehouse-conflict problem, it would not license a criticism of WHL's per-booking enumeration.

**Retention.** Cycle-11's C4 verdict stands. **Improvement.** The diagnosis is more precisely a disjunction collapse; and the scope point is that even a true strong claim about general noncomputability leaves WHL's per-booking enumeration untouched.

## 6. C5 — the cycle-11 response engaged this well, but it *understated* its own conclusion, and that understatement is the criticism

Cycle-11 said C5 is false as a universal rule: existence of a Lean predicate proves well-formedness, not faithfulness or adequacy of application, and WHL's own notes say so repeatedly. The response pointed at the file's own words:

> "The language does not assert which conjectures are true; it provides the vocabulary to state them."

That is right. But there is a sharper form of the criticism, and its absence is a defect of the cycle-11 treatment.

C5's clause ("a Lean predicate's existence proves that its name faithfully captures the source prose and that its application is adequate") is not merely false. It is *exactly the failure mode WHL's type declarations are designed to prevent*, and the WHL text makes this explicit in a specific way: the file *separates* the type from the intended meaning. `exact_duplicate` is defined by a structural conjunction; the file then *notes* that this "is represented by `exact_duplicate`: two records with all five fields equal are inert with respect to each other. The language does not say that near-duplicates are inert; they may be alternatives." So WHL's design *requires* a claim in prose ("this predicate is meant to capture x") that the predicate itself does not assert.

Notice what that means for C5 as an appraisal rule. If C5 were accepted, WHL's own separation of type from intended meaning would be an *error* — the file would be committing C5's mistake by writing down that separation. It isn't. WHL's separation is a design feature; the file explicitly calls assumption dependence, replication, physical resource sharing, and per-replica logical time "expressive limitations."

So the sharper criticism of C5 is not merely "existence ≠ faithfulness." It is: C5, if adopted as an appraisal rule, would *require WHL to be a worse language than it is* — it would classify the very fact that WHL declares its predicate/intended-meaning gap as a defect. That is a much starker way of saying C5 is wrong, and cycle-11 should have said it.

**Retention.** Cycle-11's C5 verdict stands. **Criticism of the response's C5 section.** It understated the case. The strongest ground against C5 is not just that existence is insufficient; it is that WHL explicitly *encodes* the insufficiency as a feature.

## 8. The block's central instruction, revisited: clause defect vs. application defect

Cycle-11's opening frame said these must be distinguished. Several of its sections then blurred them by treating memo C's *unendorsed clauses* as if they were proposals being *recommended* by someone. Where the clauses are in fact describing the representation's *content*, the appropriate response is one of:

(a) the clause accurately describes a clause of the representation (assess the representation's clause);
(b) the clause does not accurately describe the representation (the clause's error is memo C's);
(c) the clause maps to a *lack* — an absent distinction, which R7 and the WHL file's notes catalogue as expressive limitations, and which the memo should not treat as an endorsement of the conflation.

Cycle-11 used (a) for C4 and (b) for C1, C2, C3 and C5, but did not use (c) systematically. Case (c) is precisely the correct treatment for C1's underlying observation: WHL cannot express assumption dependence, so a criticism that treats WHL as "calling assumption rejection a refutation" is *misdirected* — but the *underlying gap* is genuinely present in WHL, as a limitation, and that limitation is what the memo's author was probably trying to point at.
