## 2. C1 — rejection of a supporting assumption as refutation

C1 asserts a rule ("our representation calls rejection of a supporting assumption a refutation") and derives a consequence ("therefore … A's conclusion has been established false for every account").

The rule half is **partly supported and mis-scoped**. R7 says: "Rejection of one supporting assumption can defeat that support route without proving its conclusion false." So the contract's own vocabulary distinguishes a *defeat of a support route* from *proof of falsity of the conclusion*. WHL's `Refutation` type is `(snap : Snapshot) (not_c : ¬ c snap)` — a refutation is of a *particular conjecture*, by exhibiting a snapshot where it fails. That is a counterexample, not a rejection of an assumption *behind* a conjecture. And RSS explicitly separates three kinds of refutation: (a) counterexample within an account's own viewpoint; (b) derivation of inconsistency within an account's own reading; (c) **demonstration that a conjecture depends on an assumption that another account rejects**. RSS calls all three refutations, but scopes them as *local to accounts* — a refutation "does not force global agreement."

So C1 is a conflation of two of these three. Type (c) — assumption-rejection as refutation — is genuinely *available* as a notion under RSS (as fallible rival prose), but even there it is explicitly local: it refutes *within the rejecting account's viewpoint*, not "for every account." Under the selected representation WHL, `Refutation` is type (a) only; assumption dependence is not primitive in WHL at all — the file's own note says: "Source assumptions are not primitive. The language can state them as conjectures or commitments, but it does not encode a notion of assumption dependence."

So C1's rule is not the selected representation's rule. WHL does not call assumption-rejection a refutation; it does not encode assumption dependence at all. RSS does, but locally. Which means C1's "therefore" does not follow even under RSS, and has no basis under WHL.

The consequence is defective on both readings. Scope: C1 is a defect in a *proposed appraisal rule*, not in an individual application. My cycle-6 treatment of B6 and my cycle-10 §3.1 already worked the correct form of this: rejecting R5 (the independence assumption) *defeats the support route* for the conditional result and leaves the conditional claim intact. C1 asserts the opposite — that assumption rejection proves the conclusion false for every account — and is contradicted by R7, by WHL's `Refutation` form, and by RSS's own locality clause.

**Verdict on C1: defective.** The defect is that the rule misnames a support-route defeat as a universal falsity. The residual sound fact — assumption-rejection can defeat a *support route* — is already R7 and already in the earlier cycles' working account.

## 3. C2 — local commitment settles global inquiry

C2 asserts: after any account commits to a conjecture, no unresolved question about it can remain anywhere. That is a metaclaim about inquiry state, and it is **defective** on multiple independent grounds.

- R7: "Rejection of one supporting assumption can defeat that support route without proving its conclusion false." A commitment by one account does not prove the conjecture; if it did, no other account could hold an unresolved question by the same right.
- RSS: "A commitment is an account's acceptance of a conjecture. Commitment is local to an account. Different accounts may commit to incompatible conjectures." RSS's inquiry state is "a collection of accounts plus a set of open questions." A conjecture remains *open* if "no account has committed to it or refuted it." So under RSS, one account's commitment does **not** close the question for other accounts; it only removes *that* account from the open-question set for that conjecture.
- WHL: the file's own note says it "does not assert which conjectures are true; it provides the vocabulary to state them." WHL has no machinery that could make a local commitment settle global inquiry; a `Commitment` is a `Prop`, verified or not in a particular reading, and nothing in `Conjecture : Type := Snapshot → Prop` globalises it.

So C2's rule is **not** the rule of either the selected representation or the rival. It is a *strawman* that neither text endorses and that would be defeated by the very apparatus both texts contain. The form of the error matters: C2 is not a defect in WHL or RSS; it is an *invented* criticism of "the representation" that no representation clause asserts. As a criticism of a language clause, C2 fails because there is no clause to criticise. As a criticism of an application — if some earlier cycle or memo had tacitly globalised a local commitment — it could be serious; but no such application is supplied here, and I do not manufacture one.

**Verdict on C2: defective as a criticism of the representation, because the representation does not assert the rule C2 rejects.** The contract R7 and RSS both explicitly reject it.

## 4. C3 — the closure claim about projections

C3 asserts: every projection must lose information; and this holds even on a singleton domain and for injective projections.

The first clause is **sound only as a claim about non-injective projections**. A projection, in RSS's usage, is "a function from a viewpoint and a reading to a single answer… always a loss of information." That sentence is itself a rhetorical claim, not a theorem; and C3's second clause correctly *refutes* it as stated: on a singleton domain, a projection is constant and the loss is zero-information-lost (nothing was distinguishable to blur); and an injective projection over the admitted domain is invertible on that domain and so loses no distinction *within the admitted domain*.

So C3 is doing two different things at once. It states a universal rule and then states the exact conditions under which the rule fails. The two clauses are inconsistent. Which clause is the claim? If the claim is "every lossy projection is lossy," it is tautological but uninformative. If the claim is "every projection losses information including injective and singleton — bearing ones," it is **false**, and C3's own second sentence supplies the counterexample.

Scope of the outcome: this is a defect in a *proposed language clause of RSS* (if "projections are always a loss of information" is read as a clause), and, more importantly, a defect in the *appraisal rule* — C3 proposes a rule ("all projections are lossy") and derives that this is a defect of the representation. It is not. The **useful** residual distinction is exactly what R6 already gives: a projection onto *bounds* loses the distinction between the exact set and the interval when interior values are unattained; a projection onto *the exact set* need not lose anything on a given domain. RSS's "always a loss" is thus a slogan that overstates a real and already-modelled issue (see R6, and cycle-5/case-A/case-B instances: {3, −2} vs [−2, 3]; {5, 1} vs [1, 5]).

I attribute to this response the reading that RSS's "always a loss of information" is rhetorically overbroad and is not a load-bearing clause; the load-bearing clause in the selected representation is R6's bound-versus-exact discipline. Memo C's C3 correctly catches the overbroad slogan but then treats the catch as a defect of the representation rather than of RSS's own wording — and in fact the slogan is RSS's, not WHL's, so C3 misattributes the target.

**Verdict on C3: half-sound, half-defective, and mis-targeted.** C3's own second clause refutes its first; the sound part reduces to R6.

## 5. C4 — omission of a conflict-resolution algorithm "proves" noncomputability

C4 asserts: a semantic proposal that supplies no conflict-resolution algorithm, by that omission alone, proves the represented problem noncomputable.

This is a **category error**, and it is worth naming why rather than dismissing it. "Noncomputable" is a property of a function: there is no Turing machine that, for all inputs, returns the correct output. "No algorithm is supplied" is a fact about the text: the specification does not *include* an algorithm. The first does not follow from the second. Nothing in RSS's own description says the problem *cannot* be decided; RSS says it *chooses not to decide*, and that its noncomputability is *deliberate*: "It is deliberately noncomputable: it does not provide an algorithm for resolving conflicts, and it treats any demand for a single answer as a projection that must be justified outside the semantics." That is a *stipulation of the semantic class*, not a theorem about the underlying question.

So C4 conflates two claims:

1. **The class RSS specifies is not a decision procedure.** True by RSS's own declaration.
2. **The underlying problem is mathematically noncomputable.** Not established. It would require either a reduction from a known undecidable problem, or a diagonal argument, or an appeal to a theorem. None is given. In fact the *selected* representation WHL does the one-thing-per-booking selection in finite time over a finite list, so the WHL version of the task is decidable-by-construction over its admitted domain (finite bag, finite greatest-revision sets, set enumeration). RSS's rhetoric of "noncomputable" is a *stance about how the semantics treats incompleteness*, not a computability-theoretic result.

This matters for the selected policy. If someone read C4 as a lemma about the "real" warehouse task, they would conclude that a decision procedure is impossible and therefore that the WHL bounded enumeration is a misrepresentation. On the supplied grounds that conclusion is not licensed. The right scope is: RSS does not *supply* an algorithm, and states this as a design property; whether the underlying question is undecidable is a separate question the text does not address.

**Verdict on C4: defective.** "No algorithm supplied" is not "noncomputability"; the RSS text itself uses the term in the design-stance sense and does not certify the mathematical claim.

## 6. C5 — a Lean predicate's existence as faithfulness

C5 asserts: the existence of a Lean predicate (its type-checking / its inhabitation / its availability as a definition) proves that its name faithfully captures the source prose and that its application is adequate.

This is the memo's most direct criticism of the representation and the one most worth engaging, because it is *nearly* right and the *nearly* is the whole point.

What a Lean predicate's existence shows is: the predicate's type is well-formed in the ambient logic, its definition is syntactically admissible, and it therefore can be *used* in statements and proofs. It does **not** show:

- that the predicate's *informal name* corresponds to the prose it is intended to capture. WHL's own file says "The language provides relations for: … exact duplication (all five fields equal) …". Whether "all five fields equal" *is* "exact duplicated delivery" for a given corpus's purposes is an *interpretation* claim that the definition does not and cannot validate on its own.
- that applications of the predicate to a particular snapshot are correct. `dominated_in rs r` type-checks, but whether it is the *right* notion of dominance for a replication-aware setting is a *semantic* choice. WHL's own notes say exactly this: "The language provides vocabulary for stating conjectures, commitments, and refutations" and "does not assert which conjectures are true."
- that the predicate's *evaluative content* matches a prose clause if the prose clause is itself ambiguous. WHL's file explicitly leaves the `reserves_effect` versus `expired_deletes_rule` divergence unresolved: "The language does not choose between them." The existence of both predicates does not adjudicate between them; a later reading must.

So C5's first clause ("existence proves faithfulness") is **false**, and C5's second clause ("and adequacy of application") is also **false**, and the correction is *not* a defect I am inventing in the WHL text — it is what the WHL file itself says, repeatedly, in its "intended meaning" and "expressive limitation" notes. C5 is thus an *appraisal rule* that, if adopted, would license exactly the move the WHL file warns against: treating well-formedness as semantic authority.

The 6th line of C5's neighborhood is worth noting: C6 — memo C's own clause — actually states the correction C5 needs: "Being able to express a false claim is not a defect by itself." C6 and C5 together already show the correct scope. A predicate that lets you state the false claim "no earlier record was ever delivered" (memo B, B2) is not defective for permitting the statement; the defect (if any) lies in the *application* that treats the statement as established. C5 seems to want a stronger criterion, but the memos themselves — including memo C — do not supply one, and the WHL file's own vocabulary does not embody one.

**Verdict on C5: defective as a universal rule.** It is overbroad; it collapses type-existence with semantic faithfulness, and the WHL text itself denies the collapse. The sound residual is exactly C6.

## 7. C6 — expressibility of false claims is not a defect

C6 is sound and, I think, correct — with one qualification. R7 already says "Reports, reconstructions and criticisms below are fallible claims," and WHL's file says it "does not assert which conjectures are true." Both texts endorse the position that the *capacity* to state a falsehood is a feature, not a bug. What C6 adds is the positive direction: "The question includes what distinctions the representation allows us to inspect, criticize and use."

That positive formulation is what WHL actually offers: structural supersession versus evaluative expiry; payload identity versus delivery provenance; exact set versus bounds; per-booking alternatives versus cross-booking coupling; source assumptions as conjectures (with the noted limitation that assumption *dependence* is not primitive). All of these are distinctions WHL allows to be *inspected and criticised*. The bound-versus-exact discipline, run concretely on cases A and B, is a demonstration that the distinction is *usable* — the endpoint-agreement in cases A and B does not coincide with the exact attainability sets.

**Verdict on C6: sound, adopted.** It is the correct register statement, and it correctly defines the question this block poses.
