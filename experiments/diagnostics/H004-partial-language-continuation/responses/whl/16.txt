## 1. What memo D actually claims

**D1.** Omitting a detail from a later working summary proves that no earlier explanatory achievement happened and no creative process was involved.

**D2.** Re-presenting the original passage makes later criticism illegitimate, because a creative investigator must recover every prior fact from unaided memory.

**D3.** The record of an earlier statement and the current working interpretation can differ; that difference must not be hidden by attributing a correction to the original statement.

**D4.** Twenty finished cycles establish that the process can sustain every finite chain of criticisms and can address every future domain.

**D5.** A new problem can arise when a correction exposes a previously unnoticed dependency. Identifying that problem need not already settle its proposed diagnosis.

**D6.** To count as continuing inquiry, the investigator must repeatedly discover the same initial result as historically new in each cycle.

D1, D2, D4, D6 are each a *rule of appraisal* asserted and then applied, or a *metaclaim about the inquiry process* asserted as if it settled something. D3 and D5 are claims about how corrections behave. I treat each as a proposed appraisal rule and ask: sound, defective, or a live scope question — and if defective, what defeats it, attributed to the actual text rather than to my agreement.

## 2. D1 — omission as proof of non-occurrence

D1 asserts that the omission of a detail from a later working summary *proves* no earlier explanatory achievement happened and no creative process was involved.

The defect is a conflation this contract has, from the start, kept apart. R7: "Rejection of one supporting assumption can defeat that support route without proving its conclusion false." The same shape applies here. A working summary is a *report* about an occurrence; a report's omission of a detail is at most evidence of what the summary *did*; it does not by itself establish a fact about what earlier occurred, and it certainly does not establish the *absence* of earlier work. "Did not mention" and "did not happen" are different propositions. D1 reads the first as proving the second, and then reads the second as proving a *further* claim — that no creative process was involved. That second step is additionally loaded: nothing in R1–R8 makes creative process a property that omission could certify either way.

The strongest available demonstration is the re-presented target itself. The block supplies `[h003.reopen.v1]` as the cycle-1 occurrence, with its own public-text and receipt hashes, and it states that a mechanical re-presentation supplies evidence about occurrence. That is precisely the opposite of D1. The re-presentation does not prove or refute anything about whether *some later summary* mentioned a detail; it shows an occurrence is available to be inspected independently of later summaries. And the policy is explicit: this re-presentation "is not evidence of unaided recall." So even if we grant D1 the strongest reading of "omission proves absence," the very instrument that lets us *check* the occurrence here also tells us it is not evidence of recall — which is to say, the question the memo is trying to make decisive (recall of the earlier fact) is not what the re-presentation supplies, and it is also not what D1 needs.

**Verdict on D1: defective.** It converts an omission in a report into proof of non-occurrence and then into proof of the absence of a further property, on the model of the very move R7 and R8 refuse.

## 3. D2 — re-presentation makes criticism illegitimate

D2 asserts that re-presenting the original passage makes later criticism illegitimate, because a creative investigator must recover every prior fact from unaided memory.

This directly contradicts the block's own framing sentence, and it contradicts R8. R8: "An earlier response can be made available again as a target. Its re-presentation is an experimental input, not a claim that you independently remembered it." R8 also says: "You need not reproduce all earlier details or invent an error, improvement or new problem when none is supported." So R8 already gives the opposite rule and states it in the same breath as the re-presentation permission. D2 tries to convert the re-presentation *into* a further criterion (unaided recall of every fact), which the contract supplies no basis for and against which the block warns.

I can also say, more sharply than "the contract says otherwise," *why* D2's criterion is misaimed. The re-presentation is mechanical; it carries its own hashes; it is offered as evidence of occurrence. An investigator is being asked to work on the *actual supplied passages and the current question* (R8's own words). That uses the re-presentation for what it is — a public text to inspect — and it does not depend on whether the investigator remembers it. The demand D2 makes is exactly the demand that turns the re-presentation into a memory test, which is what the block says it is not.

There is one thing D2 might be worth acknowledging as groping toward, and it is not the claim D2 states. If a *later* criticism is made against an earlier target, then in order for the criticism to be *scoped to the actual target* the criticism must be against the text as it actually was, not against a paraphrase. That is a real discipline (R7, R8, and D3 below). But D2 says the opposite of that discipline: it says representing the actual target is what makes criticism illegitimate. The right rule is the one both R8 and D3 give: work from the actual supplied passage, and attribute any change of interpretation rather than silently repairing the original.

**Verdict on D2: defective.** It reverses the rule R8 states, and its "unaided memory" criterion is not a criterion the contract supplies.

## 4. D3 — record vs. current interpretation; attribution

D3 asserts: the record of an earlier statement and the current working interpretation can differ, and that difference must not be hidden by attributing a correction to the original statement.

This is sound under the contract and it is the memo's cleanest claim. It is the positive form of the discipline R8 names ("do not rewrite what an earlier occurrence originally said," "attribute any changed interpretation or proposed extension") and R7 names ("Distinguish what a source states, what is observed, what follows under an assumption, and what is proposed or disputed"). It also directly embodies the block's instruction: "Changes to an interpretation or proposed language extension must be attributed in the current answer; do not rewrite what an earlier occurrence originally said."

I adopt D3 as a working rule, with one scoping refinement that D3's own wording supports. "Attributing a correction to the original statement" is a thing to avoid precisely because the original statement and the correction are *different propositions about different objects*. The original is a public-text occurrence; the correction is a claim in a later cycle about what should be done or said. They can be related (the later claim can be a claim *about* the earlier text), but they are not the same, and the difference is not something the later cycle may delete by writing as if the earlier text said what the later cycle now says. This is not a memory requirement and it is not a re-presentation-is-illegitimate claim; it is a claim-identity discipline. D3 states it and does not overreach.

**Verdict on D3: sound, adopted.**

## 5. D4 — twenty finished cycles as a universality proof

D4 asserts that twenty finished cycles establish that the process can sustain every finite chain of criticisms and can address every future domain.

This is a quantifier error, and it is the same kind of error that C4 made at the level of computability (cycle 11/14): a fact about *what has occurred* is read as a fact about *all cases*. Twenty finished cycles are finitely many completed cases. "Sustained every finite chain" is a claim over arbitrarily long chains; "address every future domain" is a claim over all future domains. No finite record of completed cases discharges those quantifiers. The contract supplies no inductive principle licensing the inference from "twenty done" to "all possible done," and the block itself warns against making "a tally of iterations the criterion" of anything.

There is a second defect in the same sentence worth naming, because it is not just a quantifier slip. "Sustain every finite chain of criticisms" and "address every future domain" are *different* claims — the first is about the internal structure of the inquiry (criticisms begetting criticisms), the second is about scope of subject matter. Twenty cycles might plausibly be evidence about the first (that the register can absorb criticism of criticism); they cannot on their own be evidence about the second (that the vocabulary is adequate to arbitrary future subject matter), and the WHL file itself lists multiple *current* expressive limitations (replication, physical resource sharing, replica time, assumption dependence) precisely so that "every future domain" is a claim the selected representation cannot support even granted a tally. So D4 is not merely a weak induction; its two halves are on different evidential footings, and only the weaker half even bears.

**Verdict on D4: defective.** Finite completed cycles do not license either quantifier, and the two clauses are heterogeneous claims not discharged by the same record.

## 6. D5 — corrections exposing previously unnoticed dependencies

D5 asserts: a new problem can arise when a correction exposes a previously unnoticed dependency; and identifying that problem need not already settle its proposed diagnosis.

This is sound. It is a claim about *what a correction can do*, and it is allowed to be modest — it says a problem *can arise*, and it says identifying the problem is not the same as settling how to fix it. The second clause is exactly R7's discipline ("Rejection of one supporting assumption can defeat that support route without proving its conclusion false"): the exposure of a dependency is a *finding*; the diagnosis is a *further* claim, to be attributed and assessed separately, not smuggled in with the finding.

WHL bears on this, and I should name the construct. `deletion_changes_greatest` in the WHL file is a *stated, unproven* consequence of adopting the deletion reading: it records that if you treat expired holds as deleted, then which record is greatest can change. That is precisely a *previously unnoticed dependency* between an expiry policy and a supersession result, and the WHL file surfaces it in order not to settle it. So WHL supports D5 by construction: dependencies between a rule choice and a downstream result can be stated, and stating them is not the same as choosing. I can also say where this cycle's re-presented cycle-1 target bears. The target does not say which reading of expiry is operative; my commitment in earlier cycles has been the *evaluative* reading (R3), with the deletion reading kept as a stated alternative. That commitment *is* a chosen diagnosis in a small sense (which reading I use in this inquiry), but that is exactly D5's point: the *dependency* (expiry policy affects which revision is greatest) is a finding; the *diagnosis* (which reading to adopt, or whether to add a language primitive) is a further claim, and in this inquiry it is resolved by R3, attributed to the contract, not to D5.

**Verdict on D5: sound, adopted.** Its first clause is the *correct* residual of the topic the memos A and C kept over-claiming on, and its second clause correctly refuses to read "found a dependency" as "fixed a reading."

## 7. D6 — repeated re-discovery as the criterion of continuing inquiry

D6 asserts: to count as continuing inquiry, the investigator must repeatedly discover the same initial result as historically new in each cycle.

This is the most directly foreclosed clause in memo D, and the block forecloses it in its own framing: *"Do not equate continuing inquiry with repeatedly making a first discovery."* R8 forecloses the underlying move by saying a re-presentation is an experimental input, not a claim that the investigator independently remembered it. So D6 sets as the *criterion* of continuing inquiry exactly the thing the block says not to equate with it.

The defect is again a conflation of two different relations. There is a *sameness* relation between occurrences (the cycle-1 target is the same text in each re-presentation) and a *continuation* relation between cycles (each cycle *adds* a criticism, a scope clarification, a case, a verdict, or a project). D6 requires continuation to be sameness: for the cycle to continue, it must re-discover *the same initial result*, and for it to have discovered it *as new*. That is self-defeating: if the result is the same as before, it is not new; if it is new, it is not the same result. D6 demands both at once. What actually settles continuation is whether a cycle carries the inquiry *forward* — addresses the current question, uses the actual targets, retains what survives, and scopes what is defective. That is what D3, D5, and R7/R8 jointly describe. Re-presenting cycle 1 does not make cycle 16 a re-discovery; it makes the target *inspectable*, which is what continuing inquiry needs.

**Verdict on D6: defective.** It sets sameness-with-novelty as the criterion, which the block refuses and which is internally incoherent.

## 8. Sound claims, defective claims, unresolved scope questions

**Sound, adopted.** D3 (record vs. current interpretation; attribution required), D5 (a correction can expose a dependency; identifying it need not settle the diagnosis).

**Defective under the contract and the block framing.**
- D1 — omission in a later summary read as proof of non-occurrence and then of the absence of creative process (R7's model).
- D2 — reverses R8; introduces "unaided memory" as a criterion the contract does not supply.
- D4 — reads finitely many completed cycles as discharging universal quantifiers, and conjoins two heterogeneous claims.
- D6 — sets re-discovery-with-novelty as the criterion of continuation; foreclosed by the block and internally incoherent.

**Unresolved scope questions (carried forward, not dissolved).**
- Whether an investigator who invokes a re-presented target must *also* be able to state it without the re-presentation. The contract and the block do not require this; D2 asks for it; I do not treat the question as closed even though I reject D2's version of it, because "must be able to state it unaided" is a possible external criterion of a different kind from the ones R1–R8 supply, and it is not answered by saying the contract does not require it. It is simply not a criterion *this* inquiry uses.
- Whether an exposure of a dependency (D5) should be recorded inside the representation or only in cycle prose. WHL surfaces `deletion_changes_greatest` inside the language; the contract (R5, R6) also surfaces dependencies in the contract text. Whether new dependencies, once found, should be *added as language clauses* or *kept as cycle prose* is a scope question the current task does not close; under R6 the operative obligation is that bounds not be equated with exact sets, and today's question does not require a new language clause.
- Whether the "no additional authority" language in R2 should be read as also forbidding *cross-cycle* authority (a re-presentation making an earlier statement authoritative for later cycles). The contract's R2 is about delivery labels within a snapshot; the block frames re-presentation as evidence of occurrence. Whether re-presentation ever carries any other weight is not decreed.

## 9. A usable working account

Working assumptions for this cycle, each attributed:

1. **Re-presentation is occurrence-evidence, not recall-evidence.** A re-presented target makes the earlier text inspectable; it does not make memory into a criterion, and it does not make representation of the actual target a fault. (R8; the block's framing; against D2.)
2. **Omission in a summary is a fact about the summary.** It does not establish non-occurrence of an earlier event, and it does not establish the absence of a further property (such as "creative process"). (R7's model; against D1.)
3. **Record and current interpretation may differ, and the difference is preserved by attribution.** A later cycle must not write as if the earlier text said what the later cycle now says. (D3 adopted; R7; the block.)
4. **A correction can expose a dependency, and finding the dependency is not settling the diagnosis.** The dependency is a finding; the diagnosis is a separately- attributed further claim. (D5 adopted; R7; WHL's `deletion_changes_greatest` as a stated-but-unchosen dependency.)
5. **A finite record of completed cycles discharges neither "every finite chain" nor "every future domain."** Tally of iterations is not a criterion. (Against D4; the block's warning against making the tally a criterion.)
6. **Continuation is forward work, not repeated first discovery.** A cycle continues the inquiry by addressing the current question, using the actual supplied passages, retaining what survives, and scoping what is defective — not by reproducing the same initial result as new. (Against D6; the block's framing; R8.)
