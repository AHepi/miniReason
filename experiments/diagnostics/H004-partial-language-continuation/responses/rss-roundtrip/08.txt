## 2. Criticism 1 (expiry instant / promotion) — valid as an observation, over-extended as a promotion, and resting on an unexamined premise

**What cycle 7 attributes to cycle 6.** It says cycle-6's B3 section framed the disagreement as "strict less-than vs `t ≤ expiry`" but then placed B3 on the WHL side in a way that made the interesting fact sound like a minor footnote, and that cycle 6's "I do not fold it into A1" phrasing understated a disagreement internal to the supplied representational material. **Is the target accurate?** Partly. Cycle 6 did say, in §2 (B3) and again in §4 and §5, that the WHL `reserves_effect` agrees with B3 and disagrees with R4, and it did call the item "lower-grade" and decline to promote it. So cycle 7's *description* of what cycle 6 said is essentially accurate — cycle 6 treated the item as low-grade and deferred it. Cycle 7 is not attacking a strawman on the surface.

**Do the grounds support the promotion?** Here the criticism overreaches, and this is my first substantive finding about cycle 7.

The grounds cycle 7 offers for promotion are that the disagreement is "between R4 and the WHL specimen considered as a formal resource" and is "independent of B3." Both of those are true *and were already stated by cycle 6*. Cycle 6 §2 (B3) explicitly wrote that the WHL specimen's `reserves_effect` uses `t ≤ expiry` "which is the opposite of the contract's R4 strict-less-than" and called it "a real disagreement between the WHL specimen's `reserves_effect` and the declared contract R4." Cycle 6 §5 also already recorded it as "a genuine contract/specimen disagreement about expiry strictness." So cycle 7's promotion does not add a new finding; it renames an item cycle 6 had already identified as a contract/specimen disagreement. The difference between cycle 6's "lower-grade" and cycle 7's "named" is a *labeling* choice, not a change in the underlying observation.

But there is a sharper problem, and it is one cycle 7 did not examine. The task's declared contract is "the current declared contract, not an assertion that every real warehouse follows it," and the WHL specimen is a "fallible representational resource." So the disagreement between R4 and the WHL `reserves_effect` is a disagreement between *two supplied texts*, both of which the inquiry treats as revisable. Cycle 7's promotion presents the disagreement as if it were an *internal inconsistency to be adjudicated*, but the declared material already tells us how to adjudicate: "where they disagree with this contract, expose the disagreement rather than silently changing the task." The WHL specimen disagreeing with R4 is exactly the case the declaration anticipates. Cycle 7's "promotion" is therefore not an unexpected discovery; it is the declared status of any specimen/contract divergence. Cycle 7 is right that it *should* be stated, and cycle 6 already stated it; cycle 7 is wrong to treat its statement as a correction of cycle 6 rather than a repetition in stronger voice.

**Is cycle 7's explanatory diagnosis of cycle 6 accurate?** Cycle 7 says cycle 6 "misidentified which instant B3 is about." Let me check cycle 6's actual B3 section as supplied. Cycle 6 wrote: "B3 says the hold expires at time 5; at current time 5 the hold is still active." Then: "under R4 ... at equality or later it reserves zero ... the hold is not active." Cycle 6 then treated B3's "should persist through the named expiry instant" as *an alternative reading*. Cycle 7 claims the misidentification is that cycle 6 framed the disagreement as being about whether *equality* counts, when the real structure is that B3 and the WHL specimen *agree with each other* against R4.

Reading cycle 6 again: it *did* say "the WHL `reserves_effect` (`t ≤ expiry` reserves) agrees with B3 and disagrees with the declared R4." So cycle 6 already paired B3 with the WHL specimen against R4. Cycle 7's Criticism 1 is thus largely *re-describing* cycle 6's own pairing and calling it a correction. The one thing cycle 7 genuinely adds is the framing that the interesting fact is that WHL and B3 *agree* — but cycle 6's sentence already contains that. So Criticism 1's "defect" claim — that cycle 6 misidentified the instant — is not established by the passage cycle 6 actually contains. Cycle 6 identified it correctly; it just declined to promote it.

**Outcome.** Criticism 1 is *valid as a scope-note* (the expiry disagreement deserves to be carried as a standing contract/specimen divergence, not tied to B3's occurrence) but *invalid as a defect claim* about cycle 6 (cycle 6 did not misidentify the instant or the pairing). The promotion is a legitimate editorial proposal, attributed to cycle 7, over a finding cycle 6 already recorded. What should return to the earlier account: nothing needs restoring, because cycle 6's observation stands; what should *not* return is cycle 7's suggestion that cycle 6 had gotten the pairing wrong.

## 3. Criticism 2 (B1 independence standard) — valid and important; the best criticism in cycle 7

**What cycle 7 says.** Cycle 6's B1 verdict ("two agreeing reports are one route, not two independent routes") rests on an unstated standard of evidential independence, which R7 does not supply. Cycle 7 proposes naming it as an imported assumption or weakening the verdict to "the independence asserted is not supported by the admitted provenance."

**Is the target accurate?** Yes. Cycle 6's B1 section indeed asserted that "if the two routes share the single thing that could be wrong, they are not independent routes" and then, in cycle 6's words, said the provenance "contradicts the independence it asserts." Cycle 7 correctly notes that R7 supplies no definition of evidential independence; R7's "rejection of one supporting assumption can defeat that support route without proving its conclusion false" is about *defeat*, not about *independence*.

**Do the grounds support the criticism?** Yes, and this is where cycle 7 does real work. The relevant distinction cycle 6 blurred is between (i) a *provenance* fact (Y copied from X's intermediate file) and (ii) an *epistemic* verdict (the two reports are not two independent routes). Going from (i) to (ii) requires a theory of when two evidential routes are independent — a common-cause or no-shared-failure-mode standard — which neither R1–R8 nor RSS supplies. RSS *displays* the shared delivery but, as cycle 7 says, treats provenance as a set and does not rank accounts, so it supplies no evidential tier and no independence metric. Cycle 7's diagnostic is correct.

**Scope of the correction.** Cycle 7 is careful here in a way it was not in Criticism 1: it explicitly says "I retain the substance ... but I overstate the contract's reach." That is the right scope. The bare assertion "two independent routes" is unsupported by the admitted provenance; the stronger contract-flat "they are not independent" is an imported epistemic claim. Cycle 7's suggested rephrasing captures this: the conclusion is *not supported* rather than *contractually contradicted*.

**Outcome.** Valid criticism. It identifies a real gap between cycle 6's premises and its conclusion. What should return to the earlier account: the B1 verdict restated at the weaker, honest scope ("the admitted shared provenance does not support the asserted independence; support would require an independence standard not declared"), with the assumption named as imported. This is the clearest case in cycle 7 of a criticism whose grounds support its verdict and whose stated scope matches those grounds.

## 4. Criticism 3 (B5 slide from lossiness to universal falsity) — valid, and correctly bounded

**What cycle 7 says.** Cycle 6 argued B5 is defective because bounds don't fix the exact set, but B5's actual claim is that bound agreement answers *every* criticism of the represented alternatives. Those are different; falsifying "every X is answered" requires exhibiting an unanswered X, not merely showing bounds are lossy in general. Cycle 7 says the missing existence step is supplied by the cycle-5 Case A interval/set gap, so the argument can be completed, but cycle 6 didn't complete it explicitly.

**Is the target accurate?** Yes. This is a genuine logical-structure point. "Bounds are lossy" (a general property) does not entail "there exists a criticism bounds agreement fails to answer" (the specific existence claim B5's "every" requires for its falsity). Cycle 6 used the general fact as if it discharged the specific quantifier.

**Do the grounds support the criticism?** Yes. The distinction between universal falsity and general lossiness is sound and is exactly the discipline R6 imposes ("an interval may be reported as bounds but must not be equated with an exact set unless all intervening admitted integer values occur"). Cycle 7 correctly notes that the cycle-5 Case A gap (bounds [−2, 3] versus exact set {3, −2}) supplies the exhibited unanswered criticism, so the argument is completable.

**Scope.** Cycle 7 explicitly limits the correction: "The verdict on B5 stands, but on a cleaner argument than I gave." That is the right scope — it repairs the argument without inflating the verdict. RSS is correctly invoked only as corroborating the lossiness, not as supplying the existence step.

**Outcome.** Valid criticism with correct scope. What should return: the B5 argument restated so the general R6 observation *supports* the refutation rather than *constituting* it, with the cycle-5 Case A gap named as the exhibited counterexample-criticism.

**Outcome.** Valid scope note, correctly bounded. What should return to the earlier account: the B2 verdict, retained, with the explicit condition that it holds on the premise B2 actually gives and would need re-examination only if B2's reader were respecified to operate over a delivered-payload bag.
