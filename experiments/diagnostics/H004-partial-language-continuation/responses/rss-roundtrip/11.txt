## 2. Claim-by-claim investigation of MEMO C

### C1 — "Our representation calls rejection of a supporting assumption a refutation. Therefore when account B rejects an assumption used by account A, A's conclusion has been established false for every account."

This claim is about *the representation's own commitments*, and it has two parts: a description of the representation ("our representation calls rejection of a supporting assumption a refutation"), and an inference drawn from it.

The *description* misreads the sources. In the RSS specimen, a refutation is explicitly one of three things: "(a) a counterexample within an account's own viewpoint; (b) a derivation of inconsistency within an account's own reading; or (c) a demonstration that a conjecture depends on an assumption that another account rejects." RSS then says refutations are *local to accounts and do not force global agreement*. So RSS itself does **not** call the mere rejection of a supporting assumption a refutation of the conclusion; it lists assumption-dependence as one *form*, and immediately restricts the scope to the account. The WHL specimen and R7 say the same thing in different vocabulary: R7 — "Rejection of one supporting assumption can defeat that support route without proving its conclusion false." So the representation here (R7 + RSS) is, if anything, *against* the "therefore" clause of C1.

The *inference* is the defect. Even granting the description, "B rejects an assumption A used" says only that A's *support route* is defeated; it does not establish A's conclusion *false*, and certainly not "for every account." That last step — local rejection → universal falsity — is exactly the elevation R7 and RSS refuse. So C1 is defeated under the sources it claims to describe, and it is defeated at the very move the sources specifically bar: assuming that assumption-rejection = refutation = falsity for everyone.

Attribution and scope: C1 is a claim *about the representation* that the representation contradicts. It is not a claim about data; it is a claim about how a rule/refutation relation behaves, and it is wrong about that. The defect is a *clause-level* misreading of what "refutation" is in RSS and of what R7 permits to be inferred — not an application error. Method note: this is a criticism of the representation's *described* commitments, and it lands because the description is inaccurate.

### C2 — "Once any account commits to a conjecture, no unresolved question concerning that conjecture can remain anywhere. A local commitment settles global inquiry."

RSS is explicit: "A conjecture is open if no account has committed to it or refuted it. A commitment is an account's acceptance of a conjecture," and "Commitment is local to an account. Different accounts may commit to incompatible conjectures." It further says open questions "are not solved by RSS; they are carried forward." So the *local* character of commitment is a representational primitive in RSS, not an incidental feature.

C2 therefore misstates the representation's own commitment structure. But beyond misreading, C2 is *internally* wrong even taken as a claim about inquiry independently of the sources. A commitment by *one* account cannot remove an open question global to the inquiry, because another account may hold the conjecture unresolved or refuted; if a single local commitment settled inquiry globally, RSS's notion of "disagreement between accounts as a first-class object" would collapse. And this is precisely the fact the current cycle relies on: my working account retains B6's missing-coupling-policy question as *open* even while I have committed to R5's independent-admissibility scope for *this* task. Under C2, committing to R5's scope would have to settle the coupling question; under RSS and R7, it does not. So C2 is defeated both as a description of the representation and as a substantive claim about inquiry.

Scope caveat I add explicitly: C2's defeat does *not* say commitment is idle. A local commitment is what lets me compute a case *under* a chosen reading — case B's {5, 1} is computed under a committed reading. What C2 gets wrong is the leap from "I am committed here" to "no unresolved question remains anywhere." Losing that leap loses nothing I need; retaining it would illegitimately close questions that the inquiry has deliberately left open.

### C3 — "Every projection must lose information. This also holds on a singleton input domain and when the projection is injective over the admitted domain."

The first sentence is sound as a general RSS-style slogan: RSS says "a projection is always a loss of information" and RSS is described as noncomputable, i.e. it does not attempt to be information-preserving. Taken loosely, "projection is a loss" holds for the paradigm case: a projection to a single availability number discards the set of reservations, which RSS treats as "more informative than the sign."

The second sentence is the defect. It *overextends* the slogan to cases where nothing is lost. On a singleton input domain, a map to a single value discards no distinctions — there is only one input to distinguish. If the map is injective over the admitted domain, distinct inputs remain distinct in the image; nothing is dropped. So "every projection must lose information" is false as an unrestricted universal, and C3 inadvertently proves this itself by adding the qualifiers that the slogan needs. The correct scope: a projection loses information *when it is non-injective over the admitted domain with distinctions that matter for the question at hand*. RSS's "always a loss" is a loose slogan about the *paradigm* projection (single number from a set of possible reservations); it does not survive translation into a universal about all maps.

Attribution and scope: I do not thereby reject RSS; RSS's slogan need not be read as the universal C3 makes it. The misreading is C3's, and the fix is representational scope, not a change to RSS. Under the working account, the relevant projection (single availability number from {3, −2} or from {5, 1}) *is* a loss because it drops which alternative was chosen; the relevant projection (a map to an injectively encoded form) *is not*. C3's universal is defeated; its instance on the working account's actual projections stands.

Method note for the block's "clause versus application" distinction: this is a *clause-level* error in C3's formulation of RSS's slogan, not an application error in any projection I have actually used. My cycle-10 flattening worry (A2/B5) is an *application-level* misuse of the interval; C3 is the *clause-level* overuniversalization of the slogan that could excuse such misuse.

### C4 — "A semantic proposal supplies no conflict-resolution algorithm. That omission alone proves that the represented problem is mathematically noncomputable."

The first clause is a correct description of RSS: RSS is "deliberately noncomputable: it does not provide an algorithm for resolving conflicts, and it treats any demand for a single answer as a projection that must be justified outside the semantics." So the omission is not a bug; it is a designed feature.

The "therefore" is the defect. "No algorithm is supplied by this *semantic class*" is a fact about RSS's expressive role, not a computability result about any *problem*. Noncomputability is a property of a *problem* under a specified class of computations; it is not established by the absence of an algorithm in a description. A problem can be trivially decidable and still have no algorithm written down in a semantic proposal; a semantic proposal that refuses to give one tells you nothing about decidability. Worse, RSS itself says it "compares readings" and treats a single answer as a *projection that must be justified outside the semantics* — a projection, in the WHL representation of the task, is exactly the kind of thing that *is* computed once a reading and an account are fixed. So the representations in play contain, not exclude, computable sub-cases. C4 conflates "this semantic class does not specify an algorithm" with "the represented problem is noncomputable."

Attribution and scope: RSS's noncomputability, as *stated*, is a refusal to force a global algorithm; that is consistent with (indeed, the WHL `possible_reservations` representation shows) *per-account, per-reading* computations. The defect is C4's inference, not RSS's design. I do not here claim to have proved the represented problem computable; I claim C4's *proof of noncomputability* fails, and the noncomputability claim remains an *unresolved scope question*, not settled. That distinction matters for the block: a clause-level overclaim, not a defect in RSS.

### C5 — "A Lean predicate's existence proves that its name faithfully captures the source prose and that its application is adequate."

Wrong, both halves. `exact_duplicate` exists as a predicate in the WHL specimen; its existence proves nothing about whether the *name* faithfully captures source prose — the WHL specimen's own header says the file "declares a language, not a translation of any particular corpus," i.e. it deliberately does *not* claim faithful capture. The specimen's notes reinforce this: "Duplicate deliveries convey no additional authority" is *represented* by `exact_duplicate`, but the language "does not say that near-duplicates are inert; they may be alternatives." So the *representation* is a chosen encoding of a source phrase and not a proof of capture, and the very boundary between "duplicate" and "near-duplicate" is a design decision in RSS/WHL, not a semantic proof.

The second half — that a predicate's existence proves its *application* adequate — is also false, and it is the block's central invitation to distinguish clause from application. `reserves_effect` exists as a function in the WHL specimen; from its existence nothing follows about whether *applying* it to a case with `t = expiry` gives the result the contract wants. Indeed the WHL specimen's `reserves_effect` and R4 *disagree* at equality (this is my standing tension (e)). So the existence of `reserves_effect` coexists with a live question about whether its application matches the source. C5's inference would erase that tension; the sources preserve it. C5 is defeated.

Attribution and scope: C5 conflates *formal existence* with *semantic and applicative adequacy*. It is a criticism of the method by which the representation's clauses are promoted to authority, and it is right to *name* that method as a risk — but wrong to assert that the existence of a predicate *proves* either thing. The correct negative statement is the one the sources already make: the specimen "does not assert which conjectures are true; it provides the vocabulary to state them." That is precisely the position the sources take, and C5's "proves" is the step they refuse.

### C6 — "Being able to express a false claim is not a defect by itself. The question includes what distinctions the representation allows us to inspect, criticize and use."

**Sound**, and this is the most important line in the memo, because it is the one that rescues the representation from over-strong criticisms of *expressiveness*. R7 says exactly this discipline: reports and criticisms are fallible claims; distinguish stated from observed from assumed from proposed/disputed. WHL's prose says the language "does not assert that any conjecture is true" — its purpose is to *state* and to *refute*, so being able to state a falsehood is its intended role, not a defect. RSS is explicitly non-doctrinal: "the language does not rank" readings. So the first sentence of C6 is correct under both specimens.

The second sentence is a correct *question* framing rather than a claim: the useful question about a representation is the distinctions it makes inspectable, criticizable, and usable. This matches R6 (report exact set, don't flatten), R2/R1 (payload vs provenance), R3/R4 (supersession vs expiry), and R7 (criticism targets). No defect. C6 supplies the operating discipline for the whole memo, exactly as A5 did for MEMO A.

Scope caveat that matters for this block: C6 licenses *using* a representation whose clauses are incomplete or whose applications are questioned; it does not license C1–C5. C6's own clause separation — expressive ability is not semantic adequacy — is the standard against which C1 (refutation = global falsity), C2 (local commitment = global settlement), C3 (projection always loses), C4 (no algorithm = noncomputable), and C5 (existence = capture + adequacy) all fail. The memo is internally split: C6 sets the correct standard; C1–C5 violate it.
