{"body":"The earlier account is largely right to treat this as coordination under change rather than a character contest. I would keep that frame but add three refinements. First, an agreement about meaning needs an interpretation rule for unforeseen cases, not just a task list. Write down what done means for the few chores that cause repeat disputes, with examples: sink empty, counters clear, bins at curb by 7pm. Add a rule that if the definition is unclear, the person doing the task chooses a reasonable reading but must flag it at the next check-in; hazards and shared costs are not left to individual discretion. Second, make revision routine. Schedules change, so any flatmate can call a ten-minute reset; until the reset, the current plan stands with a named fallback. This prevents silent drift and also prevents one person from reopening every agreement constantly. Third, address avoidance one-to-one, not as a group confrontation. Ask what makes the conversation costly and offer formats: written updates, pairwise talks, a shorter meeting, or a neutral facilitator. Do not treat absence as consent. At the same time, do not let one person's refusal to discuss become a veto over the others' need for a workable flat. If someone will not participate, set a temporary minimum that does not impose new costs unilaterally: each person handles their own dishes, laundry, and room; shared chores follow a visible default rotation or are suspended until agreement, except for health and safety. If standards differ sharply, say so. The person with the higher standard may need to do more, trade other chores, or agree to pay for help; equal minutes are not always the fair answer. Finally, if repeated attempts fail, mediation or a conversation about whether the living arrangement still works is legitimate. The aim is not perfect harmony but fewer repeated disputes and a way to revise the deal when life changes.","commitments":"I am taking up the claim that this is primarily a coordination and communication problem, but only as a working hypothesis. It would be defeated or complicated if the avoidance turns out to be about fear, resentment, power, or something outside the flat. My proposed next move is a short written charter for the few recurring chores plus a review date, not a comprehensive household ledger. That depends on at least two people being willing to try a low-cost structure, and on no one being unsafe in the conversation. A proposed test: for two to three weeks use a shared note that states chore, done-definition, owner, fallback and review date, and hold a ten-minute check-in; relevance is to see whether ambiguity or follow-through is the main obstacle. Success would be fewer disputes about what was agreed and at least one missed task handled without a fight. If that fails, I would move to one-to-one inquiry about the avoidance, then a neutral facilitator or a discussion about changing the living arrangement; that depends on lease terms, money, willingness, and whether anyone is being harmed. I am not assuming a cleaner is affordable, that all three want to stay, that standards are equal, or that resentment can be waved away. I would not impose paid help or new costs without consent. A criticism to keep in view: a shared board can become surveillance or a moral scoreboard, so it should stay task-focused and be reviewed for whether it reduces or increases tension. Unresolved: what the avoidance means, whether prior agreements already feel punitive, and whether the higher-standard person will accept less, do more, or pay for help.
