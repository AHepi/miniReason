The pattern you describe—agreement, later disagreement about what was agreed, avoidance—suggests that the content of the agreement is never externalized. Each of you leaves the conversation with a different internal model of what was decided, and there is no shared artifact to which any of you can point when the models diverge. The work schedules make it worse because they keep changing the parameters the agreement was supposed to govern, and the avoidance is a predictable response to an interaction that reliably produces friction without resolution.\n\nWhat I would do first is stop treating the chore conversation as a negotiation to be won and start treating it as a recurring meeting with a written output. Concretely: propose a short recurring check-in—fifteen minutes, weekly—whose sole purpose is to update a shared list. The list should have columns for task, owner, and the time window in which the owner is responsible. The time window matters because your schedules change; 'kitchen by Sunday evening' is a different commitment from 'kitchen sometime soon.' When someone cannot meet their window, the move is to say so before the window closes, not to explain afterward why they could not. That single norm—notice before breach—prevents most of the retrospective arguments about what the agreement meant.\n\nThe avoidance is the harder part. If one friend is skipping the conversations, forcing attendance will not help. But you can reduce the cost of attending by making the meeting short, predictable, and focused on updating the list rather than on airing grievances. If the avoided conversation is specifically about renegotiating the division—perhaps because that friend feels outvoted or overburdened—then a one-on-one before the group meeting can surface that without an audience. What you should not do is let the group drift into an implicit arrangement where the people who show up do the work and the person who avoids gets a pass. That is stable in the short run and corrosive in the long run.\n\nI cannot tell from what you have described whether the disagreement is about fairness, about memory, or about differing standards of cleanliness, and those call for different responses. The written list helps with memory and with fairness, less with standards. If standards are the real issue, you will need an explicit conversation about what 'clean enough' means, perhaps with examples. That conversation is uncomfortable but bounded; the alternative is a low-grade ongoing dispute that never gets resolved because its object is never made specific.\n\nOne more thing: you are one of three, and you are the one asking.
