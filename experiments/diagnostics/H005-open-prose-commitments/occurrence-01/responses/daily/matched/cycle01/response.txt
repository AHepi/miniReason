That admission is the most load-bearing thing on the table, and it is also the point where I think the contributions stop short.

What I would actually take up is the rival's comparison step, but with an important revision. The rival asks each flatmate separately what they think has been going wrong, then looks at whether the answers overlap. The objection identifies what is wrong with treating the asker's report as diagnostic evidence: the report is a compression, and both diagnoses and demurrals are built on it. The same worry applies to the answer the other flatmates give. A person asked 'what has been going wrong?' will produce a narrative, and that narrative is itself a compression, selected for the audience and for the moment. Overlap between three compressions is not evidence that the underlying situations are the same; it may just mean the three people have converged on the same public story. And non-overlap is not evidence that the situations differ; it may just mean the three people are selecting different parts. The rival's check is worth doing, but it should not be read as settling the diagnostic question it was introduced to settle.

So a modest revision: ask the one-on-one question, but ask it about concrete instances rather than about characterizations. 'What was the last chore-related thing that bothered you, and what happened?' rather than 'what do you think has been going wrong?'. Concrete instances are harder to select from in the same way, because the question fixes the referent. Three answers to a concrete-instance question give more purchase on the three-way distinction than three answers to an open characterization question. This is a proposal, not a validated method. I do not know that it works, and the cost the rival names — three separate conversations, each of which can become a grievance session — is unchanged.

On the written list itself: I think the objection is right that writing down 'cadence and standard' does not settle what the standard should be, and I think the account understates this. A written list makes the disagreement legible and portable, which is a real gain when the alternative is re-arguing from memory. But making a disagreement legible is not the same as resolving it, and the objection's worry about converting an implicit quarrel into a written one is not answered by saying the list is revisable. Revisability helps if the parties are already willing to revise; it is not what makes them willing. I would keep the list as a proposed artifact but stop treating it as the load-bearing move. The prior account says the structural-ambiguity claim carries the proposal; the objection says that claim is overstated; I think the honest position is that the proposal survives as an aid but not as a solution, and the diagnosis it was supposed to rest on is not established.

What I can say from the exposed material is that a flatmate who does not respond to a circulated draft is the central case, not the edge case, because the procedure only invokes the draft when someone raises the issue, and the person most likely to raise it is the person not avoiding.
