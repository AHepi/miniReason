{"body": "The account's central move is to re-describe a practical problem as a scoping problem, and then to locate the reported disagreements in an ambiguity between task assignment, shared standard, and scheduling expectation. I want to press on that move on two fronts, because I think the diagnosis may be narrower than the account presents it, and because the account's own stated commitments contain a tension it does not resolve.\n\nFirst objection: the ambiguity claim is doing more work than the material licenses. The account says the disagreement is 'almost guaranteed if the agreement only fixed (a)', but the flatmates report that they agreed on who would do something and later disagreed about what the agreement meant. That is consistent with the assignment/standard ambiguity, but it is equally consistent with a simpler story: they agreed on who would do a task, and the disagreement afterwards is about whether the person is doing it at the standard or frequency the other expects. That second story is not a different ambiguity that the written list resolves; it is a disagreement about performance, not about specification. Writing down 'cadence and standard' does not settle what the standard should be; it only makes the disagreement explicit. So the written-list proposal may convert an implicit quarrel into a written quarrel, which is not obviously progress.\n\nSecond objection: the account treats the avoiding flatmate as a procedural problem — a body whose absence can be routed around via written circulation. But the commitments already concede that if the avoidance is a refusal rather than a preference about format, the proposal does not address it. That concession is correct, and I think it is worse than the account admits. Circulating a draft and inviting low-cost written objection addresses someone who is present but reluctant to speak. It does not address someone who has withdrawn from the shared project of living together. If that is what is happening, the first draft may be read as a fait accompli, which can harden rather than soften the withdrawal. The account wants to avoid diagnosing the avoidance, and I agree with the restraint, but the restraint undercuts the proposal: the proposal is only appropriate for some of the cases the account itself says are indistinguishable from the material.\n\nThird objection, aimed at the scoping move itself. The account frames the original question as not well-posed, but 'What should I do?' is a perfectly ordinary practical question, and refusing to answer it in favor of scoping is itself one kind of answer — one that places the burden back on the asker to reformulate. The account half-acknowledges this in its commitments, calling the scoping move potentially avoidance in its own right and saying it has no clean reply. I do not think that is a small residual; I think it is the main cost of the contribution, and the account undersells it by listing it last.\n\nWhat survives, in my view, is not nothing. The distinction between assignment and standard is a real distinction, and the suggestion to separate 'assigned' from 'done' from 'acceptable' is a useful vocabulary whether or not it resolves the underlying disagreement. The repeated-review idea is sound in principle. But the claim that the ambiguity is 'structural' and that 'no amount of goodwill will fix the pattern' is a stronger claim than the reported material supports, and it is the claim that carries the proposal. If that claim is wrong or overstated, the proposal is overbuilt for what it can do.\n\nWhat I would put in place of the account's framing is a smaller one: the first move is to find out which of three situations obtains — a specification disagreement, a performance disagreement, or a withdrawal from the shared arrangement — and the account has already told us, correctly, that the material cannot distinguish these. If the material cannot distinguish them, a written list is a bet on one of the three. If that hardening effect does not occur, or if withdrawn flatmates typically respond to written drafts by re-engaging in low-cost writing, the objection is misplaced.\n\nMy third point — that the scoping move is itself a substantive answer with costs — is close to what the original account already concedes in its own commitments. I am not sure whether stating it again constitutes an independent criticism or merely an agreement with an admission that the account already made.
