  "body": "The question asks what happens to information about the physical state of matter that forms a black hole when the black hole evaporates. In classical general relativity, the black hole horizon hides the infalling matter, and the exterior spacetime after formation is described by a metric that depends only on mass, charge, and angular momentum. All other details of the matter are inaccessible from outside, so the classical picture suggests information is lost to the exterior. However, Hawking's semiclassical calculation shows that black holes emit thermal radiation with a temperature inversely proportional to mass, and the radiation carries no correlations that would encode the initial state. If the evaporation is complete, the final state appears to be purely thermal, implying that the initial information is destroyed. This conflicts with quantum mechanics, where the evolution of a closed system is unitary and information is preserved. The conflict is the black hole information paradox.\n\nSeveral proposed resolutions exist. One is that information is encoded in subtle correlations in the Hawking radiation, perhaps through quantum gravitational effects that modify the semiclassical picture. Another is that a remnant survives, holding the information, but this raises issues with infinite production of remnants and their entropy. A third is that the black hole does not completely evaporate, but leaves a stable Planck-scale remnant. Yet another is that information is lost and quantum mechanics must be modified, but this is generally considered unpalatable. More recent ideas include the holographic principle and the firewall paradox, which suggests that if information escapes, an infalling observer might encounter high-energy radiation at the horizon, violating the equivalence principle.\n\nMy current view is that the most promising approaches involve quantum gravitational corrections that make the evaporation process unitary, with information encoded in the radiation. This likely requires a deeper understanding of how gravity and quantum mechanics combine, perhaps through string theory or loop quantum gravity. The problem remains open, but I lean toward information preservation, as unitarity is a cornerstone of quantum theory. However, I do not claim a definitive answer; the paradox highlights the need for a theory of quantum gravity.",
