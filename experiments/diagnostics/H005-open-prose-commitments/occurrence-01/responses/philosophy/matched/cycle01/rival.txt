{"body": "The original account frames the issue as a matter of registers (causal versus rational shaping) and proposes ownership of stance as the locus of responsibility. I want to try a different cut: treat responsibility as a practice of holding accountable rather than a property of the agent's relation to motive.\n\nStart from the observation that the question 'is she responsible?' is almost never asked in a vacuum. It is asked by someone, or some institution, considering whether to blame, punish, demand an account, or adjust expectations. On the practice view, the answer is not discovered by inspecting the causal history of motive and not settled by checking whether the agent has taken a stance; it is negotiated against the purposes the questioner has and the norms of the exchange. That is not a reductive claim that responsibility is arbitrary. Practice norms can be better or worse, and there are reasons to have practices that leave room for excuse and practices that forbid certain excuses.\n\nThis reframing changes what the original problem looks like. The forces-that-shaped-motives problem becomes: under what conditions should a holding-accountable practice treat a motive's provenance as a valid excuse? The answer will be plural. Some practices (criminal liability in a liberal state) should be more provenance-sensitive than others (a parent's reproach, a friend's demand for explanation). The ownership account tries to give one criterion for all; the practice account expects different criteria and accepts that they may conflict.\n\nA second move: distinguish holding responsible from being responsible. The first is an activity, the second a status. The original account plausibly conflates them by locating responsibility in the agent's stance. On the practice view, the status may be thin or absent while the practice of holding remains warranted, for forward-looking reasons (deterrence, repair, maintaining a relationship), and vice versa. That is not a concession that anything goes; it is a claim about what kind of question we are answering.\n\nI am not proposing this as a replacement. The ownership account has real attractions, especially its account of manipulation and coercion. The pressure point it identifies \u2014 whether stance-taking has unchosen determinants of the same kind \u2014 is less threatening on the practice view, because the practice does not require a metaphysically self-originating stance. But the practice view has its own pressure: it risks licensing cruel or arbitrary holding-accountable, and it needs constraints on which practices are legitimate. I do not have those constraints fully in hand.\n\nA concrete comparison case: an adult raised in a coercive sect who now, in a new context, fails to recognize an obligation she was never taught. The ownership account asks whether she can take a stance on the motive. The practice account asks whether the current holders are entitled to demand an account from her, and on what terms. Both are useful, and they are not the same question.", "commitments": "I am committing to the claim that 'responsibility' in the original problem is ambiguous between a status and a practice, and that the practice reading is at least as central as the status reading for the cases the problem cares about. That is a claim, not a conclusion, and it is criticizable by showing that the status reading does all the important work or that the practice reading collapses into a prediction about social response with no normative content.\n\nI am also committing to the view that the provenance of a motive should be treated differently by different holding-accountable practices; that is, I am rejecting any single criterion (ownership, ex nihilo authorship, or otherwise) as the answer to the original question. That rejection is a consequence of the practice framing and stands or falls with it.\n\nWhat I propose to take up, if this direction is carried further: (1) spelling out constraints on legitimate holding-accountable practices, since without them the practice view looks permissive and arbitrary; (2) working the sect example carefully to see whether the practice view gives a worse or better result than the ownership view; (3) checking whether the manipulation and coercion cases, which the ownership account handles well, are still handled by a practice account that expects plural criteria. These are proposals, not findings; each could fail.\n\nI am explicitly not committing to the practice view as the correct resolution. I am not claiming the original ownership account is wrong; I am treating it as one frame among others. The unresolved problem I would flag as most serious is that the practice view may be unable to say when a holding-accountable practice is illegitimate without importing exactly the agent-relative criteria it was trying to avoid. If that turns out to be so, the practice view is a supplement, not a rival."}
