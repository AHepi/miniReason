{"body": "The prior account tries to dissolve the puzzle by separating a motive's causal origin from the agent's stance toward it, then conceding that the stance itself may be unchosen. My rival worry is that this separation may be doing less work than it appears to. If the stance — endorsement, resistance, identification, indifference — is itself a disposition with a causal history the agent did not author, then 'the agent's own practical standpoint' is not a second thing alongside unchosen forces; it is one more of them. The regress is not blocked by calling responsibility 'a practice of holding each other to reasons.' That descriptions tells us what we do; it does not tell us whether doing it is warranted when the responsiveness the practice presupposes is itself luck-shaped.\n\nSo I would pose the problem differently. Instead of asking whether responsibility survives unchosen motives, ask what work the word 'responsibility' is doing in a given context, and who benefits from the stopping point chosen. In blame, the stopping point is usually the agent's present responsiveness. In excuse, the stopping point is the causal chain behind the motive. Both are selective. The asymmetry the prior account notes — coercion excuses, temperament does not — may track not a deep fact about responsibility but a practical need to keep certain people answerable and others not.\n\nA more deflationary proposal: treat 'responsibility' not as a property that either survives or vanishes under causal scrutiny, but as a standing in a normative relationship. Whether someone is responsible is then relative to a practice, an audience, and a purpose — holding someone to account, assigning a task, distributing blame, granting excuse. On this reading, the question 'does responsibility survive?' has no single answer, because the term shifts with the practice. What survives or fails to survive is a particular entitlement to demand, resent, or sanction. Some of those entitlements may be defensible even when the underlying motive is unchosen, because the entitlement rests on present capacities and social roles rather than on ultimate self-authorship. Others may not.\n\nThe cost of this move is that it gives up the idea of a single metaphysical verdict. If you wanted responsibility to be a fact about the agent independent of our practices, this account will look like an evasion. If you accept that responsibility-talk is practice-embedded, the hard question becomes which practices are justified, and that question cannot be settled by conceptual analysis of 'motives' and 'choice' alone. It requires asking what we are trying to do when we hold someone responsible — deter, repair, protect, express, discipline — and whether the unchosen origin of the motive defeats that aim.\n\nAgainst my own proposal: it risks making responsibility too easy for the holder and too cheap for the held. If responsibility is just a standing in a practice, then any practice that finds it useful can generate it. The prior account's conjecture, by contrast, at least tries to name a condition — reason-responsiveness plus attributability — that could fail. My reply is that the condition is still there, but it is a condition on the practice's legitimacy, not on the agent's metaphysical state. That may be a distinction without a difference if practices are all we have.
