{"body": "I take the account to be roughly right in its central move—separating origin from stance—but I think its self-diagnosis of a 'causal-regress objection' misnames what is actually doing the work in the strongest version of the objection, and that this matters for whether the proposed response succeeds.\n\nThe account characterizes the regress as pressing an 'ultimacy requirement' and replies that the objection 'gains force only if one has already accepted an ultimacy requirement.' That reply is too quick for a structural reason. The strongest form of the difficulty is not that every grounding feature traces back to an unchosen cause, so that no stopping point is non-arbitrary. The difficulty is that the account's positive criterion—responsiveness to reasons plus attributability to the agent's practical standpoint—is itself a feature of the agent, and the account has not said why that feature escapes the provenance treatment it applies to motives. If unchosen motives are admitted because they are the agent's, and a practical standpoint that is unchosen is admitted because it is the agent's, then the admission rule looks like: whatever the agent's standpoint endorses is attributable, whatever it does not endorse is provenance. But that is close to a tautology—it tells us the agent is responsible for the actions the agent is responsible for—and it silently reproduces the very stopping point the account claims to avoid defending. The regress worry is not that a stopping point is arbitrary; it is that the account's stopping point is not argued for but presupposed by the way it defines the standpoint.\n\nThere is a second, less advertised cost. The account offers a graded responsibility picture for boundary cases but does not say what the grades track. If they track degree of reason-responsiveness, they slide toward capacity, and capacity is exactly the kind of constitutive unchosen feature the account wants to demote to 'part of the agent.' If they track degree of identifiability with the motive, they inherit the first problem. If they track degree of availability of alternatives, they become an opportunity-for-avoidance criterion in disguise, and the account has to say whether the relevant alternatives are natural or socially provisioned. Each option is defensible, but the account does not choose, and its ecumenical spirit hides the choice.\n\nNeither of these points refutes the stance-first framing. Coercion and temperament really do behave differently in our responsibility practices, and that asymmetry is real evidence, as the account says. My objection is narrower: the account's own defense against the regress objection is doing less work than it claims, and its graded proposal is doing less specifying work than its presentation suggests. Fixing that would require an independent argument for why the practical standpoint is the right stopping point—not the claim that ultimacy requirements are optional, which leaves the stopping point in place without justifying it.\n\nI also want to flag a dependency in the disambiguation commitment. Distinguishing attributability, accountability, and liability is useful, but it does not follow that the original question is merely ambiguous among them. It may be that attributability in the absence of the others is not a distinctive kind of responsibility at all—that we call an action the agent's because we already stand ready to hold the agent to something—so that the three senses are not separable in the way the disambiguation presupposes.
