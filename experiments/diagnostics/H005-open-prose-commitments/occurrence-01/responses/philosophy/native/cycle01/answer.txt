{"body":"Yes, but only if we stop treating responsibility as requiring ultimate self-authorship. Unchosen forces shape everyone's motives: upbringing, culture, genes, trauma, opportunity, and social position. That fact alone does not settle whether an agent is responsible. Responsibility is better understood as answerability within a normative practice: the action is attributable to the agent, and the agent has relevant capacities to respond to reasons, reflect on motives, and govern conduct. The crucial question is not whether the motive had a purely self-originated cause, but whether the forces that shaped it bypassed or disabled the agent's practical agency. Coercion, manipulation, addiction, severe deprivation, and ignorance can defeat or diminish responsibility because they undermine those capacities. When they do not, a person can be responsible for what they do with motives they did not choose, including seeking help, resisting, repairing harm, or changing conditions. This supports a scalar and context-sensitive view rather than a simple yes or no. The hardest cases are those where socialization is so deep that the agent's reflective endorsement is itself shaped by the same forces. There, appealing to a true self or authentic endorsement risks circularity. We need a normative account of adequate autonomy, and that account is contested. So the answer is yes in principle, but only with a non-originationist, practice-based, scalar account that is honest about boundary cases.","commitments":"Taking this up would involve treating responsibility as answerability rather than ultimate authorship, assessing it by degrees, and using coercion, manipulation, addiction, ignorance, and incapacity as potential defeaters. It depends on a distinction between the causal origin of a motive and the agent's present relation to it. It also depends on a substantive account of adequate reflective capacities, which I have not fully specified. If that account is circular or too weak, the view loses force in deep-socialization cases. I propose to test it against cult indoctrination, coercive control, addiction, childhood religious conditioning, and implicit bias. A serious criticism is that if all motives are unchosen in origin, my distinction may collapse into a compatibilist stipulation. A proposed application is to mitigate blame or punishment for bypassing forces while still requiring repair where the agent can act. An unresolved problem is how to distribute responsibility between an agent and unjust social conditions, and whether reflective endorsement is sufficient when it is itself an adaptive response. I do not claim this settles moral luck or the metaphysics of free will."}
