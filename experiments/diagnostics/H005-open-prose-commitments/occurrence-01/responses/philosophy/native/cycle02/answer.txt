{"body":"Yes, we can make sense of it, but not by asking whether the person authored their own motives. That question is usually unanswerable and often morally irrelevant. Responsibility is better treated as a cluster: attributability, answerability, accountability, and liability or desert. Unchosen shaping affects these differently. A motive can be unchosen in origin and still be the agent's motive at the time if it is integrated into their practical reasoning or if they can respond to reasons about it. What defeats or diminishes responsibility is not unchosen origin by itself, but bypass, coercion, manipulation, addiction, ignorance, severe deprivation, or incapacity that undermines the relevant capacities or standing. Deep socialization remains the hardest case: if reflective endorsement is itself produced by the same forces, a true-self or authentic-endorsement test becomes circular. I would therefore downplay authenticity and emphasize normative competence, access to alternative norms and practices of critical response, and whether holding responsible is a fair demand under current conditions. This makes responsibility scalar and relational, and it allows answerability for repair even when blame is mitigated. I retain the earlier non-originationist and scalar practice view, but revise it by making reflective endorsement less central and by distinguishing more sharply among attribution, answerability, accountability, and desert. The account still needs a substantive theory of adequate autonomy and of distributive responsibility under injustice.","commitments":"Taking this up commits me to distinguishing causal origin from the agent's present relation to a motive, and to treating responsibility as a cluster rather than a single property. It depends on the claim that answerability can survive unchosen motive formation, which in turn depends on a non-circular account of practical reason responsiveness. I propose to test this against cult indoctrination, coercive control, addiction, childhood religious conditioning, and implicit bias, asking which capacities are bypassed and what demands are fair. A serious criticism is that if all motives are unchosen in origin, the distinction may collapse into a compatibilist stipulation that avoids the real problem. Another criticism is that a relational account may license oppressive blame when the relevant community norms are unjust. Unresolved problems include how to distribute responsibility between an agent and unjust social conditions, whether reflective endorsement is sufficient when it is itself an adaptive response, and whether liability or desert can be separated from accountability. I do not claim this settles moral luck or the metaphysics of free will."}
