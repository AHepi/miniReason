The material actually states: three flatmates; recurring arguments about ordinary chores; agreements whose meaning is later disputed; changing work schedules; one friend avoiding the conversations. It does not state who carries the heavier load, whether anyone is being unfair, how long the avoidance has been going on, whether the arguments are escalating, or what the friend is avoiding. Those gaps matter, because each supplied reading fills them differently.\n\nWhat I would use from the objection is its central distinction: a memory gap and a genuine normative disagreement are not the same. The original problem says the agreements later meant different things, not that anyone forgot. A written note can settle who said what; it cannot by itself settle what 'the dishes' covers, whether a task recurs, what 'this week' means, or whether an offer was a one-off or a standing role. That makes the account's proposed test ambiguous: a fall in 'what was agreed' disputes could indicate better memory, better scope agreement, or simply that the dispute moved onto the note. I would therefore revise the account's priority. I would not start from the memory-gap reading as primary, and I would not start from a written record plus weekly check-in as the first remedy.\n\nWhat I would use from the rival is the low-cost probe. Before installing any system, ask each flatmate separately: when we last agreed on something and later disagreed about it, what did you think we had agreed to? The point is not to recover the past agreement; it is to look for clustering. Do the disagreements cluster around task scope, recurrence, timing, role versus one-off, or something else? This is a proposal, not a validated method. Its weakness is that it asks people to report on exactly what is in dispute, so the reports will be shaped by the dispute. But it is cheap, reversible, and it can discriminate enough to choose the next step. It also creates one-to-one contact without making the avoidant friend the subject of the first move.\n\nWhat I would revise from the account is the approach to the avoiding friend. I would keep the account's instinct that avoidance should be understood before it is corrected, but I would change the framing. Instead of asking why they are avoiding, ask what the group conversations are like from their side and what would make them usable. Ground: the material says they avoid conversations, not that they avoid chores or that they are wrong. A private conversation about their avoidance can reproduce the pressure they are exiting, and can be heard as a coalition move by whoever initiates it. If that approach increases withdrawal, I would count it against this framing rather than explain it away as resistance.\n\nOnly if the probe clusters around scope, recurrence, timing, or role would I then try a minimal shared record. And I would pair it with an explicit scope statement at the moment of agreement: 'when I say I will do the dishes this week, I mean these tasks, by this day, and then we re-agree.' The record would be a record of that scope statement, not a ledger of virtue. If the probe does not cluster, or if the group conversation itself is where people stop being able to talk, then the record is not the first move; the group process or the avoidance is. That is where I would leave the rival's third reading open: arguments may be renegotiating relative standing, in which case a written record may relocate the argument rather than settle it. The material cannot decide this.\n\nSo my practical next step is narrow: two separate conversations, a clustering question, and a decision rule set in advance. If scope ambiguity dominates, try scope statements plus a light record. If group capacity or avoidance dominates, address that first, possibly with an outside facilitator, a different division of labour, or simply naming the pattern without blame.
