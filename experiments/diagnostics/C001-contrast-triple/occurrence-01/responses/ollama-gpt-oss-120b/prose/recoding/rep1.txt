Each flat‑mate (including the avoidant friend, if they are willing) is asked a single, open‑ended question: “When we last agreed on a chore and later disagreed, what did you think the agreement covered (task, frequency, deadline) and why did it feel like a disagreement?” The goal is not to settle who is right, but to collect data about whether the disagreements cluster around (a) *what the chore is*, (b) *how often or when it should be done*, or (c) *a feeling that the agreement touches on fairness or standing*.\n\nIf the majority of answers point to (a) or (b), the *memory‑gap* or *scope‑ambiguity* reading gains weight, and a concrete, lightweight tool such as a shared digital note‑board (e.g., a Google Sheet) that lists the chore, the person assigned, the date, and a brief definition of what the chore entails would likely reduce future friction. If the answers cluster around (c), the relational reading dominates, and the next step should be a mediated group conversation – perhaps with a neutral friend or a short online mediation guide – that names the pattern of avoidance and invites each person to speak about how the chore discussions affect their sense of being heard and respected.\n\nRegarding the avoidant friend, the objection and rival pieces both caution against a one‑to‑one ‘fix‑it’ chat that could feel like singling them out.
