Rather than choosing one outright, a low‑cost diagnostic step can tell us which reading is most salient for this particular flat.  The step is a brief, private interview with each flat‑mate (including the avoiding friend, if they are willing) that asks a single open question: “When you and the others later disagreed about a chore that you thought you had agreed on, what did you think the original agreement covered?”  The answers can be grouped into three patterns:\n\n* If most answers cluster around *what* was promised (task definition, frequency, deadline), the ambiguity‑of‑scope reading dominates.\n* If answers focus on *who* said what and whether they remember the promise, the memory‑gap reading dominates.\n* If answers emphasize *why* the conversation feels uncomfortable or why one person pulls away, the relational‑negotiation reading dominates.\n\nOnce the dominant pattern is identified, the intervention can be tailored:\n\n* **Ambiguity‑of‑scope** – introduce a brief “agreement checklist” at the moment a chore is claimed (e.g., “I’ll do the dishes this week, meaning washing, drying, and putting away on Tuesday”). No permanent record is needed; the purpose is to make the scope explicit before schedules change.\n* **Memory‑gap** – set up a simple shared note (a kitchen whiteboard, a phone‑shared list, or a printed chore‑log) where each agreed chore is written with responsible person and due date. The note is consulted only when a disagreement arises.\n* **Relational‑negotiation** – arrange a short, mediated group check‑in (perhaps with a neutral friend or a brief online facilitation tool) that names the pattern of avoidance and invites each person to voice what the conversations feel like, without immediately assigning chores. The focus is rebuilding a safe communication channel; any record of chores can follow later.\n\nRegarding the friend who is avoiding conversations, the diagnostic interview also serves as the first respectful contact: the interviewer frames the question as curiosity about the *past* agreement, not as a demand to re‑engage in the group chat. If the friend declines, the flat‑mates can proceed with the other two members while keeping the door open for the avoider to join the later group check‑in.\n\nIn short, the plan respects the objection’s demand for a clearer ordering of priorities, honors the rival’s caution about jumping straight to a written system, and retains the original account’s insight that making agreements concrete helps. The next step is to run the diagnostic interview, categorize the findings, and then apply the corresponding targeted remedy.\n\n**What remains open** – if the diagnostic step yields a mixed pattern, the flat‑mates may need to combine approaches (e.g., both a checklist and a shared note).
