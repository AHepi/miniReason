Two plausible diagnoses emerge. (2) The disputes are driven by ambiguity about the *scope* of the agreement (e.g., is \"dishes\" a one‑off task or a standing role?) or by an underlying negotiation of status that the chores merely express. Both readings are compatible with the facts, and the material does not favour one over the other. \n\nTo move forward without prematurely committing to a single diagnosis, I propose a three‑step, low‑cost inquiry that can be adopted, modified, or dropped based on what the flatmates actually experience.\n\n**Step 1 – Diagnose the content of the disagreement.** Individually ask each flatmate a single, open‑ended question such as, “When we later disagreed about a chore we thought we had agreed on, what part of that agreement was unclear to you – the exact task, how often it should be done, or the deadline?” The goal is to see whether the disagreements cluster around *scope*, *recurrence*, *timing*, or something else. The answers will indicate which diagnosis has more explanatory power without imposing any new system.\n\n**Step 2 – Test a minimal shared record.** If the majority of responses point to a memory‑recording issue, try a very lightweight note (e.g., a kitchen whiteboard or shared digital list) that records only three fields: *Task*, *Person*, *Date/Week*. No moral accounting or frequency rules are added at this stage – the note simply makes the agreed‑upon assignment visible to everyone at the time it is made.\n\n**Step 3 – Conduct a brief, neutral group check‑in.** Before each weekly check‑in, spend five minutes stating any schedule changes and confirming the current week’s entries on the note. Use neutral language (“Let’s make sure we all know who’s doing what this week”) and invite the avoiding friend to share any concerns about the process, not about the chores themselves. If the friend prefers a one‑to‑one conversation, the facilitator can arrange that, but the primary aim is to keep the dialogue within the group to avoid singling anyone out.\n\nAfter a few weeks, evaluate two outcomes: (a) whether the frequency of “what did we agree on?” disputes drops, and (b) whether the avoiding friend re‑engages in the group discussions. If both improve, the memory‑recording diagnosis and its remedy are vindicated.
