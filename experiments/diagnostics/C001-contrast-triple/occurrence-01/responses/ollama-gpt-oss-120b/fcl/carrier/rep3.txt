The account’s suggestion to tighten chore specifications is useful if hypothesis (1) is dominant, but the objection correctly points out that the proposed test (checking whether recurrence drops) does not separate hypothesis (1) from (2). The rival adds a valuable axis: if the withdrawing flatmate is uncomfortable with any formalised obligations, then even a well‑written chore list may feel coercive.\n\nGiven this, I propose to run *two* lightweight probes in parallel. First, create a short written note for two concrete, recurring chores (e.g., taking out the bins and wiping kitchen surfaces) that spells out what “done” looks like, when it should be done, and what the fallback is if the person cannot meet the deadline. Second, hold brief one‑to‑one conversations with each flatmate (especially the one who has been avoiding group talks) that ask, in a non‑judgmental way, how they view the flat – as a collaborative unit with shared responsibilities, or as three separate households that simply share space. The content of those conversations will indicate whether hypothesis (3) holds. If the flatmates respond that they see the arrangement as a shared unit and are willing to engage with the specification sheet, we can proceed with the chore‑specification approach. If they express that they see the flat more as independent private spaces and are resistant to any formalised joint rule, we should pause further specification attempts and explore alternatives such as personal zones, rotating duties with a default fallback, or even questioning whether the current living arrangement is sustainable.\n\nThis dual‑probe strategy respects the ambiguity hypothesis without committing to it exclusively, acknowledges the objection’s critique of the original test, and incorporates the rival’s broader household‑identity reading.
