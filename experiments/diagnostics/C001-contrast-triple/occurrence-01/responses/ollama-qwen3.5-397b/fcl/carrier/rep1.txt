Asking two flatmates separately what they think the flat is for carries conversational weight that may be premature before trying lower-stakes interventions. The rival's test—noticing whether the withdrawing flatmate engages with proposed criteria or pulls back from the idea of written arrangement itself—is more actionable and should be used alongside the specification probe.\n\nWhat I am leaving open: Which reading is correct. All three readings—ambiguity, renegotiation/legitimacy, and household-type disagreement—are consistent with what is reported. The appropriate stance is to treat them as competing hypotheses and design probes that can distinguish them, not to commit to one.\n\nWhat I would do, revised: Start with one or two concrete chores and propose written specifications, but watch the withdrawing flatmate's response to the proposal itself, not only to its content. If they engage with the criteria, the ambiguity reading gains support. If they resist the idea of written arrangements, the household-type reading gains support. In parallel, have a low-pressure one-to-one conversation with the withdrawing flatmate focused on conversational form rather than chore content. If avoidance continues after these lower-stakes attempts, then the question shifts to whether the living arrangement fits—not as a punishment for failed chore negotiations, but as a separate decision that chore disputes have surfaced.\n\nWhat I am avoiding: Presenting any single reading as the diagnosis, treating falling recurrence as confirmation of ambiguity, or deferring the household-fit question indefinitely.
